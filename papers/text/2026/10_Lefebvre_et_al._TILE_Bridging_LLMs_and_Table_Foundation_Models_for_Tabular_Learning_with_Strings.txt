ABSTRACT
Real-world tables are often heterogeneous, combining numerical features with
free-form string columns. Table foundation models (TFMs) excel on numerical
tables but require strings to be encoded numerically, while large language models
(LLMs) hold semantic knowledge but are not designed for tabular predictions.
Most existing bridges use LLMs as plug-in encoders for cell strings, yet how to
extract representations effectively for tabular learning remains underexplored. We
show that theinterfacebetween LLMs and tables matter as much as the LLM itself.
Applied cell-wise, larger LLMs do not give better features. With our interface, the
same LLMs improve steadily with size. TILE (Table-Interfaced LLM Embeddings)
combines four ingredients:row serializationto provide cross-column context,
intermediate-layer extractionto avoid layers specialized for next-token prediction,
columnar poolingto remove mean pooling’s length bias, andby-pass featuresto
route numbers and categories past the LLM. Paired with TFMs, TILE sets a new
state of the art for tables with strings. Across 100 datasets, it beats the strongest
baseline on 89% of splits, with gains across 7 LLMs and 10 tabular learners, and
ranks first on the 108-dataset STRABLE benchmark.

et al., 2026; van Breugel & van der Schaar, 2024). Yet, modeling real-world tables is challenging
because of the heterogeneity of their features. Numerical columns representing quantities sit beside
string columns carrying semantics and world knowledge, two modalities historically handled by
different learning algorithms (Grinsztajn et al., 2022; Shwartz-Ziv & Armon, 2022).
Table foundation models, particularly Prior-data Fitted Networks (PFNs) (Hollmann et al., 2023;
2025; Qu et al., 2025; 2026), now displace the industry-standard tree-based algorithms (Chen &
Guestrin, 2016; Prokhorenkova et al., 2018) on numerical tables (Erickson et al., 2025). But they are
built to model numbers. Standard pipelines therefore prepend a string encoder, such as TF-IDF, that
reduces text to surface statistics, discarding both the world knowledge carried by the strings, and the
relational context—column headers and neighbouring entries that give cell values their full meaning.
Conversely, LLMs internalize extensive knowledge about the entities mentioned in string cells
(Petroni et al., 2019; Roberts et al., 2020; Bordt et al., 2024), but bridging them with tables is not
straightforward. Naively applying LLMs to tables forces them to do numerical reasoning, at which
they struggle (Mirzadeh et al., 2025; Baeumel et al., 2025), and requires flattening the structure that
tabular algorithms exploit (Hegselmann et al., 2023; Gardner et al., 2024). Such end-to-end LLMs
are in fact outperformed by character n-grams (Figure 1), showing that LLMs alone are not enough.
Existing bridges between LLMs and tables force a choice between context and structure. Row- and
table-serialization methods (Hegselmann et al., 2023; Gardner et al., 2024; Schindler et al., 2025)
provide context but flatten the table and typically return verbalized answers rather than representations
for tabular learners. Cell-wise methods (Spinaci et al., 2025; Arazi et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2024;
2025) embed each string in isolation, which preserves the column structure but gives the LLM short,
uncontextualized inputs. The cost of cell isolation becomes clear at scale. Applied cell by cell, larger
LLMs do not give better features: across Llama3 models from 1B to 8B parameters the cell-wise
encoder barely improves, and across Qwen3 the 0.6B model beats the 8B one (Figure 4). A lone cell
such as “E.T.” remains ambiguous, so the knowledge that grows with model size stays locked in. We
show that better interfaced to tabular learning, the same LLMs improve steadily with size.
ContributionsWe call theinterfacebetween an LLM and a table the way the LLM fits into tabular
learning: what the LLM is shown, which layer is read, how token embeddings are pooled, and
which features are passed to the TFM. Prior work leaves it at its default: isolated cells in, mean-
pooled last-layer vectors out. We argue that the interface matters as much as the LLM itself, and
turn it into a design space. TILE makes four key choices, each addressing a limitation of current
practices.Row serializationgives every cell its row-level context, addressing thecell isolationthat
leaves cell-wise encoders with strings too short to disambiguate (Section 3.1).Intermediate-layer
extractionreads the LLM before the layers specialized for next-token prediction, which do not carry
the most transferable semantics (Section 5.2).Columnar poolingrestores the column structure that
serialization flattens, and removes thelength biasby which global mean pooling lets verbose columns
drown out short informative ones (Section 3.1). Finally,by-pass featuresroute numbers and exact
cell identity around the LLM, correcting its numerical imprecision and therepresentation driftthat
gives one string different embeddings across rows (Section 3.2). Together, these choices turn LLM
scale into predicitive gains: TILE’s advantage over the same LLM applied cell-wise grows from 4.2
points with Qwen3-0.6B to 16.7 with Qwen3-8B (Section 5.1). Across 100 datasets, TILE beats 30
baselines, including prior LLM-based methods and the newest TFMs (Figure 1), and ranks first on
the external STRABLE benchmark (Blayer et al., 2026), where TILE with Causilo (Cho et al., 2026)
even beats an oracle that selects the best of 51 baselines on each dataset.
2 PRIOR WORK:EXISTING BRIDGES TRADE CONTEXT AGAINST STRUCTURE
From tree-based models to foundation modelsLong dominated by gradient-boosted decision
trees (GBDTs) (Chen & Guestrin, 2016; Prokhorenkova et al., 2018) and their inductive biases
for heterogeneous features (Grinsztajn et al., 2022), tabular learning has seen a resurgence of deep
approaches (Ye et al., 2025; Holzmüller et al., 2024; Gorishniy et al., 2025) and, with them, table
foundation models (Hollmann et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025; Grinsztajn et al., 2025; Qu
et al., 2026; Grinsztajn et al., 2026; Jäger et al., 2026; Eo et al., 2026; Cho et al., 2026; Zhang et al.,
2026a; Tao et al., 2026) that beat GBDTs out-of-the-box (Erickson et al., 2025). Rather than fitting

Traditional string handling in tabular pipelinesCategorical variables are usually one-hot or

the open problem of language model encodings for text in tables (Grinsztajn et al., 2023; Mráz et al.,
2025; Lefebvre et al., 2025; Koloski et al., 2025; Kolomenko et al., 2025; Qiang et al., 2026).
Bridging language models and tabular learnersRecent work passes either isolated cells or
serialized rows to a language model. Cell-level approaches such as ContextTab (Spinaci et al., 2025),
TabSTAR (Arazi et al., 2025), CARTE (Kim et al., 2024) and TARTE (Kim et al., 2025) rely on
relatively small language models (Wang et al., 2020; 2022; Bojanowski et al., 2017), which lack
the world knowledge of much larger models. Yet cell-wise content is short and uncontextualized,
and we show that simply scaling the language model does not help it (Section 5.1). Row- and
table-serialization methods like LIFT (Dinh et al., 2022), TabLLM (Hegselmann et al., 2023), TabuLa
(Gardner et al., 2024), and others (Wen et al., 2024; Schindler et al., 2025) employ larger models but
pay for them with per-dataset fine-tuning or the LLM context limit, and verbalize predictions rather
than use a tabular learner. That cost buys little (Figure 1). TaRL (Kang et al., 2026) and Latte (Shi
et al., 2025) use LLM representations for few-shot row classification, or latent knowledge transfer
during training. The very recent CASE (Schindler et al., 2026) serializes full tables, primes the
KV cache of a Tabular Language Model (a fine-tuned Gemma 3) on a sample of training rows, and
appends its PCA-reduced last-layer embeddings to the original features. TILE similarly combines
LLM representations with a tabular learner, but serializes rows, uses an off-the-shelf LLM, extracts
intermediate-layer representations, and pools them column-wise.

3.1 THELLM–TABLE INTERFACE:CONTEXT,DEPTH,AND POOLING
Row serialization cures cell isolationStrings in tables are often very short, such as the few words
of a movie title, and short strings are ambiguous: an LLM can resolve them only given context. “E.T.”
alone could be a time zone; next to “Spielberg” in a table about movies, it is the 1982 film (Figure 2).
TILE therefore contextualizes each string within its row by row serialization (Hegselmann et al.,
2023): each row becomes a sequence of blocks “The <column name> is <cell value>,” so that the
LLM’s own attention conditions each cell on the column headers and on the rest of the row.
Intermediate-layer extraction avoids the output layer’s specializationContrary to prior work
(Hegselmann et al., 2023; Gardner et al., 2024), we do not ask the LLM to verbalize an answer, but
extract a representation of its input for a tabular learner. The last layer of a decoder LLM is optimized
for next-token prediction, and the first layers still encode surface form; intermediate layers carry
more transferable semantics than either end (Rogers et al., 2020; Skean et al., 2025). TILE reads one
layer per LLM, chosen once (14 of 32 for Llama-3.1-8B; Section 5.2).

3.2 BY-PASS FEATURES ADDRESS THE BLIND SPOTS OFLLMEMBEDDINGS
The projected representation u summarizes the row as the LLM reads it, with two blind spots:
numbers are carried imprecisely, andrepresentation driftmakes a string’s embedding depend on its
row. These columns stay in the sentence, which is what contextualizes the strings, but TILE also
routes them around the LLM asby-pass features, giving ˜x= [u;x num;x cat;x te].

The numerical by-pass preserves exact access to numbersTable foundation models excel at
processing numerical features (Erickson et al., 2025), whereas language models handle numbers
unreliably (Mirzadeh et al., 2025; Baeumel et al., 2025). Routing numerical columns only through
the LLM would give the TFM a degraded version of the features it handles best. TILE instead also
passes them directly to the TFM: the LLM contributes its understanding of the strings, informed by
the numbers, while the TFM keeps exact access to the numbers themselves.
The categorical and target-encoding by-passes expose cell identityRow-level context makes
TILE’s representations informative, but it also causes representation drift: the same string receives
different embeddings in different rows, which hides exact-value repetitions and their association
with training targets. TILE therefore gives the TFM two more views of the string columns. String
columns with sufficient repetition are target-encoded out of fold (Appendix D), and low-cardinality
ones (below 50 distinct values; Section F.3) also enter as native categorical features.
4 EXPERIMENTAL STUDY: TILEAGAINST30BASELINES ON100TABLES
4.1 EVALUATION PROTOCOL: 100TABLES WITH STRINGS
Experimental setupWe evaluate TILE on 100 tables containing strings, drawn from TextTabBench
(17), CARTE (51) and WikiDBs (32), with 65 regression and 35 classification tasks (Appendix A). For
each dataset, we sample ntrain training and 1,024 test samples (or all remaining ones), over 5 random
seeds, and report standard errors of the mean, or 95% bootstrap intervals for Elo (Appendix B).
Metrics and score normalizationWe use R2 (clipped at 0) for regression and ROC-AUC for
classification. To aggregate across datasets of varying difficulty, we min-max normalize the scores
per dataset and seed (Grinsztajn et al., 2022), over a fixed pool of 17 baselines (Appendix E) and the
three default TILE variants, so that scores remain comparable across figures. Elo ratings (Figure 1)
and raw scores (Appendix H) do not depend on this pool. TILE’s few settings—the extraction layer,
the feature-capacity window and the cardinality threshold—were set once on this benchmark and kept
fixed; each sits on a broad plateau (Figure 5, Figure F.1, Figure F.2), and STRABLE (Section 4.3)
validates them on separately assembled tables.
BaselinesWe compare TILE with table foundation models—TabPFN-2.5 (Grinsztajn et al., 2025),
TabPFN-3 (Grinsztajn et al., 2026), TabPFN-3.5 (Jäger et al., 2026), TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026),
EXAONE-Tabular (Eo et al., 2026) and Causilo (Cho et al., 2026)—and with tuned RealMLP
(Holzmüller et al., 2024), tuned XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016), CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al.,
2018) and Ridge, each fed with one of two Skrub string encoders.TF-IDF( StringEncoder)
represents each column with character n-gram TF-IDF features followed by SVD. TheLLM encoder
embeds each cell independently, mean-pools its last-layer representations, and reduces each column
to 30 PCA components (Grinsztajn et al., 2023); for a fair comparison, it uses Llama-3.1-8B,
TILE’s default backbone. We also compare with methods that handle strings themselves: CatBoost,
ContextTab (Spinaci et al., 2025), TabSTAR (Arazi et al., 2025), TabLLM (Hegselmann et al., 2023)
and CASE (Schindler et al., 2026). TabPFN-3.5, EXAONE-Tabular and Causilo are not part of the
normalization pool. Appendix E details all implementations.
4.2 TILEESTABLISHES A NEW STATE-OF-THE-ART FOR TABLES WITH STRINGS
TILE is the best performer across all training sizesTable 1 reports results at three training-set
sizes, and Figure 1 summarizes the comparison at ntrain = 1,024 with Elo ratings. Every TILE
variant ranks above every baseline in all six columns, ahead of tuned gradient-boosted trees, CASE,
and every cell-wise pipeline built on the same learners. At 1,024 training samples, the best baseline,
LLM encoder + Causilo, scores 77.8 in regression and 81.0 in classification, against 91.6 and 90.4
for TILE (Causilo). In Elo ratings, TILE with Causilo leads the strongest baseline by 333 points, and
beats it on 89% of splits (Appendix B). Raw differences are smaller in classification (89.7 against
88.6 AUC), where ROC-AUC saturates near 1 on many tasks (Appendix H). The regression gap
shrinks with training-set size, from 13.8 points at 1,024 samples to 9.1 at 10,000 and 7.3 at 50,000
(over fewer datasets), likely because with more data learners rely less on external knowledge.

4.3 EXTERNAL VALIDATION: TILERANKS FIRST ONSTRABLE
For a further evaluation varying datasets and protocol, we use STRABLE (Blayer et al., 2026), a
separately assembled benchmark of 108 string-carrying tables and 445 string-tabular pipelines. Only
15 of its tables share a source dataset with our benchmark, and those are preprocessed differently
(Section A.6). The four TILE variants take the first four places (Figure 3; raw scores in Table H.5),
ahead of TF-IDF with Causilo and a cell-wise Qwen3-Embedding-8B fed to TabPFN-2.5. TILE
with Causilo even beats picking the best baseline on each dataset. The ranking reproduces what we
observe on our own datasets: with a TFM, TF-IDF and cell-wise LLMs perform comparably, and
end-to-end models trail both. TILE also leads string representations absent from our benchmark (E5,
Jasper, All-MiniLM, FastText), providing largely independent evidence that its gains generalize.

5.4 ROW CONTEXT AND THE NUMERICAL BY-PASS CARRY MOST OF THE GAIN
Table 2 measures the contribution of each component of TILE, and evaluates variations that were
not retained. The rows above the default remove a component; those below add to or modify it; the
bottom rows give the cell-wise LLM encoder. The three learners agree; we quote TabPFN-2.5.
The semantic and the by-pass paths compoundRemoving the numerical by-pass is the most
damaging ablation, even though the LLM still reads the numbers. Conversely, omitting numerical
values from the serialization also hurts, even though those columns reach the learner through the
by-pass. Neither path substitutes for the other: the LLM needs the numbers to interpret the strings,
and the learner needs them exactly for its predictions. Pooling embeddings of the numerical cells as
well, however, does not help: within the TFM’s feature-capacity window, they come at the cost of
compressing the string embeddings further. On the semantic path, masking inter-column attention
causes the second-largest drop, and reading the last layer instead of layer 14 costs 0.5 points in
classification and 3.6 in regression, confirming the probing results of Section 5.2 on the full pipeline.
Standardization, the feature-capacity window and the target-encoding by-pass each bring less than a
point. None of these components alone explains the gap to the cell-wise LLM encoder, which shares
TILE’s numerical by-pass: masking inter-column attention, the largest, costs 3.0 (classification)
and 6.5 (regression) points, against a gap of 8.5 and 17.3. Giving the LLM encoder TILE’s other
by-passes narrows the gap only to 7.7 and 15.0, and sizing its PCA with the feature-capacity window
leaves it at8.9and16.9(both widen it with TabPFN-3).

6 DISCUSSION:UNLOCKINGLLMWORLD KNOWLEDGE FOR TABLES
Building the right interface between LLMs and tablesPairing an LLM with a strong tabular
learner, TILE turns strings into predictive signal, with sizable gains over the state of the art (Figure 1,
Table 1). Our results suggest that what has held back LLMs on tables is their interface to the table.
With row-level context and a well-chosen layer, TILE makes LLMs’ gains grow with their size
(Figure 4). TILE is versatile and improves every LLM backbone (Figure 4) and tabular learner
(Figure 6) we evaluate, and gains from advances in both, as the newest TFMs show.
Limitations and future workTILE targets tables whose strings carry predictive signal; on purely
numerical tables, a standalone TFM suffices. Its LLM embedding stage needs a GPU, though
truncation nearly halves its memory (Section G.3). Row serialization is bounded by the LLM’s
context window; this was never a constraint in our benchmark, whose longest serialized row is 3,425
tokens against the 128k-token context of Llama-3.1-8B, but very wide tables with long texts would
need chunking. Finally, the causal mask of decoder LLMs makes representations depend on column
order, which bidirectional encoders (BehnamGhader et al., 2024) would avoid: a natural next step.

ETHICS STATEMENT
By relying on LLMs, TILE may internalize and propagate the societal biases present in their
pretraining data (Bender et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2025). In sensitive applications such as hiring, lending,
or criminal justice, these encoded biases could produce unfair predictions or exacerbate existing
demographic disparities, making pre-deployment audits crucial. Conversely, by improving predictive
performance on heterogeneous tables, TILE could positively impact critical tabular-driven domains,
such as healthcare, where patient records frequently intermix clinical measurements with free-text
notes or diagnoses (Johnson et al., 2016; Miotto et al., 2016).

A EVALUATION DATASETS
A.1 DATA SOURCES
To evaluate tabular learning with strings, we assemble a diverse benchmark of 100 tabular datasets
from three sources with distinct characteristics and inclusion biases: TextTabBench (Mráz et al.,
2025), CARTE (Kim et al., 2024), and WikiDBs (V ogel et al., 2024).
TextTabBench and CARTE are established benchmarks for tabular learning, providing real-world
tables with varied text features, from short entity names to longer descriptions. Each table is associated
with a predefined prediction task (regression, binary, or multi-class classification). WikiDBs is a large
corpus of over 1.6 million semi-synthetic tables generated from Wikidata. To create meaningful tasks
from this source, we first filtered for tables with at least 1,200 rows, then manually curated a subset
of 32 tables for which we could define a relevant prediction problem. Table A.1 summarizes the final
distribution of tasks across the three sources. Table A.2, Table A.3 and Table A.4 provide further
details on each individual dataset.

A.2 DATA PREPROCESSING
We adopt the original preprocessing from TextTabBench and CARTE. For WikiDBs, we apply a
procedure similar to TextTabBench. We also ensure that multi-class classification tasks have at
most 10 classes, each with at least 105 samples. For all 100 datasets, we then apply the following
preprocessing pipeline: (1) we log-transform regression targets with wide-ranging distributions; (2)
we downsample majority classes in multi-class problems to create balanced datasets; and (3) we
discard any table with fewer than 1,050 rows post-processing to ensure sufficient data for evaluation.
We also exclude one dataset from TextTabBench with excessively long text entries, as detailed in
Section A.4.
A.3 DATA CHARACTERISTICS
Table A.5 reports aggregated features across datasets, broken down by data source. In particular, it
shows that TextTabBench tables tend to contain longer text fields while CARTE and WikiDBs tables
gather shorter string entries.
A.4 EXCLUDED DATASET
The dataset excluded from our benchmark is the OkCupid table of TextTabBench, a multi-class
classification task with 59,946 rows, 28 string columns and very long text entries. The exclusion is a
constraint of the baselines, not of our method: the cell-wise LLM encoder runs out of memory on it,
so no LLM-encoder pipeline ran on this dataset, and including it would leave holes in the comparison.
TILE processes it without difficulty, as do the TF-IDF pipelines.
For completeness, Table A.6 reports the methods that do run on all 101 tables. Adding this dataset
leaves the ranking unchanged. In absolute terms it is a hard task for every method, with raw ROC
AUC scores ranging from 65.0 to 73.2, the best being TILE with Causilo.

A.6 OVERLAP WITHSTRABLE
STRABLE (Blayer et al., 2026) and our benchmark were assembled separately, but both draw on the
small pool of public tables with meaningful text, so some sources appear in both. Of STRABLE’s
108 tables, 15 (about 14%) share a source dataset with CARTE or TextTabBench; the remaining 93
are unique to STRABLE.
Shared sourcesTen of the shared tables come from CARTE: AI/ML salaries, beer ratings, chocolate
bar ratings, CLEAR corpus, SCImago journal ranking, Michelin, ramen ratings, whisky, Wine
Enthusiast, and Yelp. A sixteenth match is possible if CARTE’s Kaggle museum table, a repackaging
of the IMLS Museum Universe file, is counted. Six come from TextTabBench: beer ratings (also
in CARTE), CFPB complaints, Kickstarter, Mercari, OSHA accidents, and San Francisco building
permits. Two of the TextTabBench-shared sources, OSHA and San Francisco permits, are paired
with a different prediction target in STRABLE: injury severity and construction cost, rather than task
regularity and approval time. Several nominal matches are distinct datasets: STRABLE’s California
Housing is the 1990 census table, not TextTabBench’s text-rich listings, and TextTabBench’s wine
and Spotify tables have no STRABLE counterpart.
Shared tables are not identical copiesSTRABLE applies uniform minimal preprocessing, with
no dataset-specific feature engineering. It keeps the most recent snapshot of each source and applies
a skewness-minimizing target transform. CARTE, by contrast, applies hand-crafted per-dataset
transformations, such as recoding whisky price brackets and binarizing its target.
The two benchmarks are largely, though not fully, independentOn STRABLE’s side, the
overlap is small relative to its size and concentrated in its Food, Commerce, and Education domains.
On ours, the shared sources account for 17 of the 100 tables, or 18 if the museum table is counted: 11
of the 51 CARTE tables, as Wine Enthusiast gives both a price and a rating table, and 6 of the 17
TextTabBench tables. This overlap reflects the scarcity of public tables with meaningful text rather
than a derivative design. Results on STRABLE can thus be read as largely complementary evidence
to those on our benchmark, with the caveat that a model’s gains on the ∼15 shared sources should
not be counted as independent confirmation across benchmarks.

B ELO RATINGS
Figure 1 summarizes the benchmark with Elo ratings, computed with the implementation of
TabArena (Erickson et al., 2025), which follows Chatbot Arena (Chiang et al., 2024).
Pairwise comparisonsEach method m is evaluated on dataset d with 5 random train/test splits
(seeds) s, the same for every method, giving an error em,d,s = 1−R 2 for regression and 1−
ROC AUC for classification. The unit of comparison is abattle: for every dataset d, seed s and pair
of methods (a, b)that were both run on(d, s), method a wins if ea,d,s < eb,d,s, loses if ea,d,s > eb,d,s,
and a tie counts as half a win for each (ties occur in fewer than 0.01% of battles). Each battle has
weight 1/5, so that every dataset has the same total weight. Because a battle only compares two
methods on the same split, regression and classification datasets can be pooled into a single rating
without ever comparing R2 with ROC AUC. Over the 36 methods and 100 datasets this gives 303,345
battles.
Rating modelThe ratings rm are the maximum-likelihood estimate of a Bradley–Terry
model (Bradley & Terry, 1952) on the Elo scale,

Unlike the online Elo update, this estimate uses no K-factor and does not depend on the order of the

defined up to a common shift, which we fix so that the mean rating over methods is 1000.
InterpretationBy Equation 3, only differences between ratings matter. A gap of ∆ points translates
into an expected head-to-head win probability: ∆ = 100 gives 64%, ∆ = 200 gives 76% and
∆ = 400 gives 91%. The +333 Elo gap between TILE (Causilo) and the strongest baseline (LLM

of compute constraints, we ran LLM encoder + EXAONE-Tabular only atntrain = 1,024, the training

Confidence intervalsError bars are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals. We resample the 100
datasets with replacement (all seeds of a drawn dataset are kept together), refit the ratings on the
battles of the resample, and repeat this 200 times (bootstrap random seed 0). The bars go from the
2.5% to the 97.5% quantile of the bootstrap ratings and are drawn around the point estimate fitted on
the full benchmark, which is why they can be asymmetric. Resampling datasets rather than individual
battles accounts for the correlation between battles on the same dataset, so the intervals reflect
uncertainty over the choice of datasets. Per-method intervals do not directly give the uncertainty of a
difference, so we bootstrap the gap itself with the same resamples: the gap between TILE (Causilo)
and LLM encoder + EXAONE-Tabular has a 95% interval of [272,397] and is positive in all 200
resamples.

D TARGET ENCODING OF THE CATEGORICAL STRING COLUMNS
Alongside its LLM embeddings, TILE passes every suitable string column to the tabular learner as a
target encoding (Micci-Barreca, 2001). This section specifies that step; Section 3.2 motivates it.
EncodingFor a string column taking the value c, classification with K target classes encodes c by
the class proportions of the training rows carrying it, ˆpk(c) =n c,k/nc: one column per target class,
or the single ˆp1(c) for a binary target, since the proportions sum to one. Similarly, regression uses the
category meann −1
c
P
i:ci=c yi, and adds a single column. Missing entries form their own category.
Out-of-fold estimationTraining rows are encoded from five folds so that a row never contributes to
its own value (the rows of one fold use the statistics of the other four). Without that, a high-cardinality
column would encode every singleton category to its own label, and the learner would read the
target off the encoding. Test rows are encoded from the whole training split. Categories seen at test
time but absent from the training rows fall back to the prior: the global target class proportions for
classification or the global target mean¯yfor regression.
Which columns are encodedA string column is target-encoded when more than 10% of its
non-missing entries hold a value that occurs at least twice. Below that the column is almost entirely
singletons and would encode to a near-constant.
Integration in the pipelineThe encoded columns are appended as numerical features to the PCA-
reduced embedding and to the table’s own numerical and low-cardinality categorical columns, and
are not themselves passed through the PCA. This path is independent of the categorical by-pass: a
column with fewer than 50 distinct values also reaches the learner as a native categorical feature,
whereas a high-cardinality column reaches it only through its embedding and its target encoding.

E BASELINES AND THEIR IMPLEMENTATION
We compare against three families of string handling. (i) Learners with internal string handling:
CatBoost, ContextTab and TabSTAR. (ii) TF-IDF followed by an SVD, and (iii) the LLM encoder
(Llama-3.1-8B applied to the cell content), each combined with seven tabular learners: Ridge,
XGBoost, CatBoost, RealMLP, TabPFN-2.5, TabPFN-3 and TabICLv2. This gives 17 baselines, all
of which ran on the full benchmark (65 regression and 35 classification datasets) with 5 seeds.
These 17 baselines, together with the three default TILE variants (using Llama-3.1-8B as LLM
backbone), form the reference pool used to normalize the scores reported throughout the paper
(Section 4.1). We additionally report TabLLM (Hegselmann et al., 2023), a fine-tuned LLM that
predicts by generating text. Since it is a classifier and runs out of memory on one classification
dataset, it covers only part of the benchmark and does not enter the reference pool. The concurrent
CASE (Schindler et al., 2026) is reported outside the pool as well, and so are three recent table
foundation models: TabPFN-3.5 (Jäger et al., 2026), EXAONE-Tabular (Eo et al., 2026) and Causilo
(Cho et al., 2026).
Every baseline sees exactly the splits TILE sees: the same five seeds and the same training and test
rows (Section 4.1), stratified for classification, and scored with the same metric code, ROC AUC
(one-vs-rest, weighted) for classification andR 2 for regression.
Learners with internal string handling CatBoost(v1.2.10) is run on the raw table with the string
columns declared as cat_features: columns are typed with a skrub TableVectorizer
(cardinality_threshold=0, high_cardinality="passthrough") so that no col-
umn is one-hot encoded and every non-numeric column is passed to CatBoost as a categorical
feature, with missing entries mapped to the string "nan".TabSTAR(Arazi et al., 2025) is the
official tabstar package (v1.1.15) with its default pre-trained checkpoint; numeric columns are
kept numeric, datetimes are decomposed with skrub’s DatetimeEncoder, and all remaining
columns are cast to text so the model sees them through its text encoder.ContextTab(Spinaci et al.,
2025) is the official SAP release sap-rpt-oss (v1.0.1) with default settings, on tables passed
throughskrub’sCleaner.
TF-IDF string representationsEvery non-numeric column is embedded independently with
skrub’s StringEncoder (v0.7.0), a character n-gram TF-IDF followed by a truncated SVD.
The SVD width is 30 components per column, reduced to ⌊500/nstr⌋ when the table has more
than 17 string columns, to keep the representation of wide tables tractable. The resulting matrix is
concatenated with the numeric columns and handed to each of the seven tabular learners listed below.
LLM encoderThis baseline is skrub’s LLMEncoder (formerly TextEncoder). Each string
cell is embedded on its own by Llama-3.1-8B (meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B) loaded through
sentence-transformers (v5.2.0); embeddings are computed once per unique cell value and
broadcast back to the table. Each column’s 4096-dimensional block is then reduced to 30 dimensions
by a column-wise PCA fitted on the training rows, concatenated across columns and with the numeric
columns, and fed to the same seven learners. This baseline is the natural ablation of TILE: the same
language model, but applied at full depth to isolated cells without inter-column attention.

F ADDITIONAL EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
F.1 THE IMPROVABILITY METRIC
The cost analyses report theimprovabilityof Erickson et al. (2025), the fraction of a method’s error
(1−R 2 for regression, 1− AUC for classification) that would be removed by switching to the best
available method on each dataset, averaged over datasets and seeds. A method that is best on every
dataset has an improvability of0%.
F.2 THE FEATURE-CAPACITY WINDOW OF EACH LEARNER
How many features can a table foundation model actually digest? The number of features used
to pre-train PFNs is usually much lower than the number of dimensions of an LLM embedding
(Tajjar et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026), and being transformers they likely inherit the shortcomings of
attention with large contexts (Liu et al., 2024). To measure this, we sweep the number of components
of a single PCA applied to a row’s concatenated cell embeddings, so that the sweep sets the learner’s
total feature count directly, independently of the table’s width.
Figure F.1 shows a clear trade-off: too few components and the projection discards information, too
many and the learner is handed more features than it handles comfortably. Between the two, each
curve has a broad plateau rather than a sharp optimum. We define a learner’sfeature-capacity window
as the range of PCA dimensions over which its mean normalized score over all tasks remains flat,
that is, within about one point (0.01) of the learner’s optimum. This gives [100,300] for TabPFN-2.5,
[50,300] for TabICLv2, [50,250] for TabPFN-3, [75,300] for TabPFN-3.5, [75,250] for EXAONE-
Tabular and [75,400] for Causilo. These windows are what TILE sizes its per-column PCA against
(Section 3.1); the number of components per column is left at its default unless the resulting feature
count would fall outside the window of the learner at hand.
F.3 CARDINALITY THRESHOLD FOR CATEGORICAL COLUMNS
TILE passes low-cardinality string columns to the tabular learner as native categorical features in
addition to target encoding and serializing them. The cardinality threshold therefore controls how
much information is represented by exact category identity.
Figure F.2 sweeps the threshold from 5 to 100 over the 100 datasets, for TabPFN-2.5, TabICLv2 and
TabPFN-3. The curves are mostly flat for all three learners and both problem types, so the threshold
matters little. We use 50 by default, but other values would serve as well.
F.4 CHOOSING THE POOLING STRATEGY
Figure F.3 compares three ways of pooling the token embeddings of Llama-3.1-8B into a row
representation, at every layer: mean pooling over the whole sequence, the embedding of the last
token, and columnar pooling. As in Section 5.2, the representations are scored with Ridge on the
embeddings alone, without the by-pass features, on the 27 probing datasets of Section A.5. Columnar
pooling is ahead at every layer, and at layer 14 it reaches 0.85, against 0.72 for mean pooling of
the last layer, the default of the sentence-transformers library; we therefore use it in TILE. This
comparison favors columnar pooling in one respect: Ridge is applied without PCA, so columnar
pooling hands it one embedding per column, an input M times wider than the single vector of mean
or last-token pooling, and part of its advantage may come from that extra dimensionality.

G COMPUTE AND MEMORY COSTS
G.1 HARDWARE AND TOTAL COMPUTE
All experiments were conducted on a high-performance computing cluster. Model training and
evaluation, including TILE and all baseline methods, were executed using different hardware. The
TILE model can typically be run with a single NVIDIA V100 GPU (32GB VRAM) and 15 CPU
cores.
In total, this project consumed approximately 15,200 GPU compute hours. This total consists of
an estimated 6,500 GPU hours dedicated to developing our method, running ablation studies, and
conducting exploratory research, alongside 8,700 GPU hours for the evaluation and hyperparameter
tuning of the baseline models.
G.2 RUNTIME BY TASK
Figure G.1 plots improvability against median runtime per dataset, separately for each problem type.
TILE holds the accurate end of the Pareto front in both: with Causilo, TabPFN-2.5, TabICLv2 and
TabPFN-3 in regression, and with Causilo, TabICLv2 and TabPFN-3 in classification. The TILE
variants take 26 to 45 s per dataset (median). TF-IDF pipelines hold the cheap end of the front, with
Ridge, then Causilo and TabPFN-3.5. In classification, CASE with EXAONE-Tabular and several
LLM-encoder pipelines also reach the front. The best baseline sits at 18% improvability in regression
and 21% in classification, against 2% and 5% for TILE with Causilo. Of the error that separates the
best existing pipeline from the per-dataset best, TILE thus removes nearly nine tenths in regression
and about four fifths in classification.
Two caveats apply to these runtimes. First, our LLM encoder embeds each unique cell value only
once and broadcasts the embedding back to the table (Appendix E), an optimization that skrub does
not implement; without it, the LLM encoder is several times slower than TILE. Second, TILE and the
LLM encoder ran on V100 GPUs, whereas CASE, limited by memory, ran on A100 and H100 GPUs,
so its lower runtimes are largely an artifact of the faster hardware.

I MODELS,SOFTWARE AND LICENSES
TILE is an architecture rather than a model, and thus relies on existing, publicly released artifacts.
We list here everything we used, with its source, so that our results can be reproduced.
Language modelsAll backbones are downloaded from Hugging Face. From the
Llama family (Grattafiori et al., 2024) we use Llama-3.1-70B and Llama-3.1-8B
(meta-llama/Llama-3.1-70B and meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B), under the Llama 3.1
Community License, and Llama-3.2-3B and Llama-3.2-1B ( meta-llama/Llama-3.2-3B
and meta-llama/Llama-3.2-1B), under the Llama 3.2 Community License. From the
Qwen3 family (Yang et al., 2025) we use the three base checkpoints Qwen/Qwen3-8B-Base,
Qwen/Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B-Base, released under the Apache 2.0
license. Llama-3.1-8B is the default backbone of TILE, and is also the model behind the cell-wise
LLM encoder baseline.
Table foundation models and tabular learnersTILE and the encoding baselines are evaluated on
top of TabPFN-2.5 (Grinsztajn et al., 2025) and TabPFN-3 (Grinsztajn et al., 2026), from Prior Labs
(https://github.com/PriorLabs/TabPFN), and TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026) ( https:
//github.com/soda-inria/tabicl). The other learners are RealMLP (Holzmüller et al.,
2024), used through pytabkit (https://github.com/dholzmueller/pytabkit), XG-
Boost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016), CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018), and Ridge regression from
scikit-learn.
End-to-end baselinesContextTab (Spinaci et al., 2025) is used through SAP’s sap-rpt-oss re-
lease (https://github.com/SAP-samples/contexttab), TabSTAR (Arazi et al., 2025)
through the tabstar package (https://github.com/eyalbd2/TabSTAR), and TabLLM
(Hegselmann et al., 2023) through the authors’ code together with the t-few recipe it builds on; we
run it with T0-3B (bigscience/T0_3B).
SoftwareThe string-encoding baselines and all table preprocessing use skrub (https://
skrub-data.org), whose StringEncoder, LLMEncoder and TableVectorizer we
rely on throughout, together with scikit-learn, sentence-transformers for the cell-
wise embeddings, and PyTorch and Hugging Facetransformersfor the LLM forward passes.
Apart from the Llama checkpoints, which carry the community licenses quoted above, every model
and library listed here is distributed under a permissive open-source license (Apache 2.0, BSD-3-
Clause or MIT). We use each of them for research purposes and in accordance with its terms.
