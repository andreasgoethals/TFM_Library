ABSTRACT
Tabular foundation models (TFMs) are increasingly popular for their strong predictive performance.
Many also provide posterior predictive distributions (PPDs), whose reliability remains less studied.
We assess reliability through point prediction, distributional quality, marginal and subgroup calibra-
tion, and interval efficiency. We then introduce TABRELIBENCH, a large classification and regression
benchmark for these comparisons and dataset-level analyses. We find that TFMs’ rankings change
between TabArena and our benchmark and across reliability metrics. Regression TFMs usually
achieve near-nominal marginal coverage and retain narrow intervals, but this favorable marginal
behavior does not imply valid conditional coverage. Dataset-level analyses show that classification
difficulty often accompanies poor calibration, whereas regression predictive performance is not a
reliable indicator of calibration. Ensembling improves predictive performance but does not consis-
tently improve calibration of PPDs; TFMs also benefit little from conformal post-processing, which
does not reliably resolve subgroup gaps. Reliable TFM selection therefore requires multidimensional
evaluation beyond aggregate rankings. Code is available at this link.
1 Introduction
Tabular foundation models (TFMs) (Hollmann et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025) are pretrained neural networks that make
predictions through in-context learning and have become increasingly popular because of their strong predictive
performance. Recent models extend this approach to larger scales (Qu et al., 2026; Jäger et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026;
Kong et al., 2026). Benchmarks such as TabArena (Erickson et al., 2025) have primarily compared these models by
aggregated predictive performance. These comparisons establish the predictive strength of TFMs, but many TFMs also
produce posterior predictive distributions (PPDs), whose properties are not characterized by TabArena. Evaluating
these PPDs therefore requires a broader view of reliability than predictive performance alone.
Reliability itself is multidimensional. In classification, accuracy measures whether a predicted label is correct, whereas
calibration asks whether stated probabilities agree with observed frequencies. In regression, a model can predict the
response accurately while producing intervals that cover too few outcomes. Proper scoring rules, including the Brier
score and continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), combine calibration and sharpness (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007;
Gneiting et al., 2007), but a favorable score does not reveal how a model balances them. Predictive accuracy, calibration,
coverage, and interval efficiency therefore provide complementary rather than interchangeable evidence.
Recent work has begun to evaluate these properties directly. Landsgesell et al. (2026) study the finite-sample behavior
of TFM distributional regression under proper scoring objectives, while Izbicki and Rodrigues (2026) benchmark
conditional density estimation on regression datasets and distinguish distributional accuracy from calibration. Both
studies focus on regression, leaving open whether conclusions about reliability extend to classification or vary across
benchmark collections and individual datasets.

Understanding such variation requires asking whether some datasets are difficult for nearly all models or instead favor
particular methods. Tschalzev et al. (2026) show that aggregate rankings can conceal dataset-specific model strengths.
Herre et al. (2026) use meta-features to predict model-family performance gaps on TabArena but find limited held-out
predictability. Separating a common dataset-level shift from a model-specific advantage requires treating the dataset as
the unit of analysis and therefore demands more datasets than are typically available in tabular leaderboards.
Reliability may also be improved at inference or through post-processing. TFMs commonly use ensembles, while
conformal methods provide post-hoc interval corrections (Romano et al., 2019; Guan, 2021; Hore and Foygel Barber,
2023; Han et al., 2022; Plassier et al., 2025; Zhang and Candès, 2024). How these interventions affect prediction,
marginal coverage, subgroup coverage, and interval width across current TFMs remains less well understood. We
address these needs through 4 contributions (appendix A provides a fuller comparison with related work):
• A large benchmark for tabular reliability.We evaluate modern TFMs and classical methods on 160 classification
and 81 regression datasets and separate measures of prediction, calibration, coverage, and interval efficiency.
• Comparisons across benchmarks and criteria.We compare TABRELIBENCHwith TabArena and show that the
strongest TFMs remain competitive, while their ordering changes across dataset collections and metrics and their
coverage–width profiles reveal differences hidden by aggregate ranks.
• Statistical analysis of dataset-level reliability.We show that most score variation reflects dataset differences and
that meta-features predict average performance shifts for predictive metrics and identify interpretable warning signs
for classification and regression reliability.
• Evidence on improving reliability.Matched ensemble and conformal studies show that small-to-moderate ensembles
improve accuracy and proper scores, whereas conformal calibration offers limited benefit to TFMs because many
already attain near-nominal marginal coverage.
2 TabReliBench: A Reliability Benchmark
TabArena is a widely used benchmark for comparing the predictive performance of tabular models across datasets (Er-
ickson et al., 2025). Our objective is to provide a more comprehensive comparison that includes both aggregate
performance summaries, as in TabArena, and dataset-level analyses. The dataset-level analyses ask how strongly
outcomes vary across datasets and whether differences among models depend on the dataset. They also ask whether
observed dataset characteristics predict when models generally perform better or worse and which models have relative
advantages. For these questions, datasets are the units of analysis, so the number of datasets determines the cross-dataset
sample size. TabArena contains only 51 datasets—38 classification and 13 regression—too few to fit dataset-level
regression models with many dataset-level features. Additional train–test splits can improve within-dataset precision
but cannot increase this cross-dataset sample size. We therefore construct TABRELIBENCH, a larger benchmark for
tabular reliability, and describe its datasets, models for comparison, and metrics below.
Benchmark curation.We draw candidate tasks from OpenML, which provides a broad and reproducible pool of
tabular datasets (Vanschoren et al., 2014). To make results comparable across datasets, we apply the same sampling
design to every candidate task. We evaluate each candidate dataset on five separately generated train–test splits, which
we call repetitions. The repetitions reduce sensitivity to any single partition while leaving the dataset as the unit of
analysis. In each repetition, we sample at most 10,000 observations without replacement to remain within the operating
regime of all TFMs and to control computational cost. We allocate approximately half of the sampled observations to
training and half to testing. Classification sampling is stratified so that the capped sample and both partitions retain
every class, while regression sampling is uniform. We retain only tasks for which these samples and partitions can
be constructed. Every model receives the same observations and partitions within a repetition, which makes model
differences paired at the dataset and split levels. After applying these criteria, the benchmark contains 160 classification
and 81 regression datasets, whose OpenML identifiers are listed in Appendix B.1.3.
Models under evaluation.Using these datasets and splits, we evaluate modern TFMs and established non-foundation
methods. We include 11 TFMs: 4 TabPFN versions (TabPFNv2, TabPFNv2.5, TabPFNv3, and TabPFNv3.5) (Hollmann
et al., 2025; Grinsztajn et al., 2026a,b; Jäger et al., 2026), 2 TabICL versions (TabICLv1 and TabICLv2) (Qu et al., 2025,
2026), 2 TabDPT versions (TabDPT1.1 and TabDPT1.3) (Ma et al., 2025), Causilo (Cho et al., 2026), LimiX-2 (Zhang
et al., 2026), and TabFM (Kong et al., 2026). TabICLv1 and TabDPT1.1 support classification only, leaving 9 TFMs for
regression point prediction. LimiX-2 and TabFM do not provide regression predictive distributions, so 7 TFMs enter
the distributional comparison.
We compare these TFMs with Bayesian additive regression trees (BART) (Chipman et al., 2010) and fixed and
hyperparameter-optimized configurations of RealMLP (Holzmüller et al., 2024) and XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin,
2016). BART provides a conventional probabilistic reference, while RealMLP and XGBoost represent neural-network

Accuracy and predictive R2 assess point prediction, whereas the Brier score and CRPS assess the full predictive

per-repetition calculations. Retaining the resulting profiles at the dataset level supports both aggregate comparisons of
average performance and analyses of whether models improve or deteriorate together, whether their relative performance
changes across datasets, and whether either pattern is predictable from dataset characteristics. Each subsequent analysis
specifies its estimand, unit of analysis, aggregation, weighting, and, where applicable, uncertainty procedure when the
corresponding results are introduced.
3 Reliability Beyond Aggregate Rankings
3.1 Rankings Across Benchmarks and Metrics
We ask whether the relative ordering of methods is stable across benchmarks and across measures of prediction and
calibration. For each metric, we first rank the configurations within each dataset and then average these ranks across
datasets, giving every dataset equal weight. To compare with TabArena (Erickson et al., 2025), we order the TFM
rows by their positions on its leaderboard and report their metric-specific ranks on our benchmark in Figure 1.The
highest-ranked TFMs are largely the same across the two benchmarks, but several exchange positions. This instability
may reflect benchmark-specific overfitting, although differences in the datasets and evaluation protocols provide
alternative explanations. Within our benchmark,the rankings also vary across metrics. In classification, LimiX-2 has
the lowest mean rank for accuracy, TabPFNv3.5 for the Brier score and classwise ECE, and Causilo for confidence ECE.
In regression, LimiX-2 has the lowest mean rank forR 2, TabPFNv3.5 for CRPS, and Causilo for calibration metrics.
TFMs nevertheless rank ahead of the classical methods across most metrics. In classification, all 11 TFMs rank ahead of
every baseline on accuracy and the Brier score, and all except TabDPT1.1 do so on both ECE measures. In regression,
all 9 TFMs rank ahead of the baselines on R2, and all 7 distributional TFMs do so on CRPS and the 4 calibration
diagnostics. Appendix D.2 reports the corresponding values and interquartile ranges. Practitioners should therefore
interpret TFM rankings cautiously, because the relative ordering of these models is sensitive to both the benchmark and
the metrics.

comparisons at 95%, 85%, and 80% nominal coverage. The main conclusion persists at the other nominal levels.

4 Predicting Reliability
We address this question using dataset meta-features, asking whether they predict the average performance shift
across TFMs and, separately, their relative performance on a new dataset. Herre et al. (2026) use meta-features
to predict normalized predictive-error gaps between model families or selected model pairs on the much smaller
TabArena datasets. Our analysis instead considers prediction and calibration metrics and separates dataset-level from
model-specific variation.

whereas relative TFM performance remains difficult to predict. This conclusion is robust to the evaluated models and
meta-predictor (Appendix Figure 16). Separate model-specific meta-regressions also predict absolute scores reasonably
well, particularly for predictive metrics (Appendix Figure 17). This absolute-score predictability can arise from average
dataset-level shifts and is broadly consistent across TFMs, but less so across classical baselines.

5 Improving Reliability
5.1 Ensemble Gains are Metric-dependent
TFMs commonly average multiple ensemble members at inference, with each additional member increasing com-
putational cost. We therefore examine which aspects of predictive and calibration performance improve as the
ensemble grows and whether these gains persist at larger ensemble sizes. We vary the number of estimators over
E∈ {1,4,8,16,32} for every TFM while holding the dataset splits and all other settings fixed. Within each dataset
and metric, we first average the five seeds and then report the median metric across the common set of datasets for
every model–ensemble configuration. Higher values are better for accuracy and R2, whereas lower values are better for
all other metrics. Figure 7 shows the model-specific trajectories for selected predictive and calibration metrics, while
Appendix H reports every metric.
Predictive gains are mostly front-loaded, but not uniform across metrics.Median accuracy increases from E= 1
to E= 32 for all 11 classification TFMs, and 10 of 11 realize an initial gain by E= 4 . Over the same range, the
median Brier score improves for all 11 classification TFMs and normalized CRPS improves for all seven distributional
regression TFMs (Appendix H.2.1). Median R2 is less uniform: 6 of 9 regression TFMs improve from E= 1 to
E= 32 , whereas TabPFNv3.5, TabPFNv3, and TabPFNv2.5 have lower medians at the larger ensemble size. Across
improving metrics, most gains occur at small to moderate ensemble sizes, with little change at larger sizes.
Calibration changes are smaller and model-specific.Classwise ECE and PIT–KS medians are comparatively stable or
non-monotone as the ensemble grows, and their trajectories differ across TFMs. FromE= 1 to E= 32 , classwise ECE
improves for only 5 of 11 classification models and PIT–KS for only three of seven distributional regression models.
The coverage–width trajectories reinforce this pattern: increasing E changes the operating point differently across

5.2 Conformal Gains Leave Subgroup Gaps
Because increasing ensemble size does not uniformly improve interval calibration, we next ask whether post-hoc
conformal calibration can correct the coverage deficiencies in Section 3.2. Our evaluation distinguishes marginal
coverage from worst-slab coverage. This distinction allows us to determine whether conformal calibration improves
only average coverage or also reliability within heterogeneous subgroups.
We evaluate 9 post-processors. Four global split-conformal variants use absolute-residual (SCP-ABS), normalized-
residual (SCP-NORM), signed quantile (SCP-CQR; Romano et al., 2019), and probability-integral-transform (SCP-PIT)
scores. We also consider the localized methods CalLCP (Guan, 2021), RLCP (Hore and Foygel Barber, 2023), and SLCP
(Han et al., 2022), together with posterior conformal prediction (PCP; Zhang and Candès, 2024) and rectified conformal
prediction (RCP; Plassier et al., 2025). The latter methods allow the conformal correction to vary with covariates
through localized or learned adjustment, and therefore provide a stronger test of whether conformal calibration can
address subgroup undercoverage. Across the 7 distributional regression TFMs and BART, we apply each post-processor
to frozen E= 8 predictions and compare marginal coverage, worst-slab coverage, interval width, and degenerate
outputs. Appendix G gives the data allocation, method-specific implementation, aggregation, uncertainty quantification,
and complete results across nominal coverage levels. We first use SCP-CQR as a global calibration baseline and then
ask whether covariate-adaptive methods better address subgroup undercoverage.
Marginal calibration benefits BART more than TFMs.Figure 8 compares the vanilla and SCP-CQR intervals at 90%
nominal coverage. BART begins with the largest marginal overcoverage, and SCP-CQR brings it close to 90% while
considerably narrowing its intervals. The uncalibrated TFMs already have marginal coverage close to the target, so
SCP-CQR changes their marginal coverage and widths more modestly; TabPFNv3 and TabPFNv2.5 are widened
because they initially undercover. These results suggest thatconformal calibration may be more useful for a classical
predictor with substantial marginal miscalibration than for TFMswhose intervals are already close to nominal coverage.
The marginal correction does not extend to worst-slab coverage, which remains heterogeneous and often decreases after
calibration, most markedly for BART. All 7 TFMs remain narrower than BART after calibration, so the base model
continues to determine interval efficiency even after marginal coverage is aligned. This contrast persists across nominal
levels: marginal-error reductions are largest for BART at lower targets, while worst-slab effects remain heterogeneous
and can be unfavorable for BART (Appendix Figure 30).

6 Discussion & Conclusion
Our results show why TFM reliability cannot be reduced to a single leaderboard. Rankings change across benchmarks
and metrics, and models with similar predictive performance can differ in calibration and interval efficiency. Most score
10

variation is nevertheless associated with datasets rather than average differences among TFMs, so meta-features predict
whether TFMs will generally perform well more readily than they predict which TFM will perform best.
This distinction has practical consequences. Local class overlap is a warning sign for both classification accuracy and
calibration, whereas regression prediction and calibration are associated with different dataset properties and should be
assessed separately. Larger ensembles improve accuracy and proper scores most consistently, but direct calibration
measures change less uniformly. Conformal calibration can correct substantial marginal miscalibration, particularly for
BART, yet neither global nor covariate-adaptive methods consistently remove worst-slab undercoverage; interval width
and degenerate outputs must therefore remain part of the comparison.
Our evidence is limited to the benchmark datasets, fitted configurations, and reliability criteria considered here, and
worst-slab coverage does not guarantee pointwise conditional coverage. Even so, the central conclusion is clear:
predictive accuracy, marginal calibration, subgroup reliability, and interval efficiency answer different questions.
Reliable tabular prediction therefore requires jointly evaluating these properties for each target dataset and decision.

A Additional Related Work
Tabular prediction and reliability.TFMs have become strong alternatives to dataset-specific learners (Hollmann
et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025), with benchmarks such as TabArena organizing comparisons across
tasks and model families (Erickson et al., 2025). Distributional evaluations complement these predictive comparisons.
Landsgesell et al. (2026) show that proper scoring objectives affect the finite-sample behavior of TFMs. Izbicki and
Rodrigues (2026) evaluate conditional density estimation on regression datasets and distinguish distributional accuracy
from calibration. We extend this perspective to classification and regression under a common protocol, and connect
aggregate results to dataset-level variation.
Dataset properties and model differences.Meta-learning uses properties of a dataset to anticipate algorithm behavior
(Vanschoren, 2019; Rivolli et al., 2018). Recent work shows that aggregate rankings can conceal models with distinctive
strengths on individual datasets (Tschalzev et al., 2026). Herre et al. (2026) find that meta-features provide limited
held-out evidence for predicting model-family performance gaps on TabArena. We extend this question to multiple
reliability outcomes by separately predicting when TFMs generally perform better or worse and which TFMs have
relative advantages.
Conformal calibration.Conformalized quantile regression adjusts predictive intervals using held-out conformity
scores (Romano et al., 2019). Local weighting, score rectification, and posterior mixtures extend this approach toward
more adaptive coverage (Hore and Foygel Barber, 2023; Han et al., 2022; Plassier et al., 2025; Zhang and Candès, 2024).
We evaluate these methods as post-processors of TFM and BART predictions, measuring marginal error, held-out
subgroup error, width, and degenerate prediction sets on matched observations.
B Benchmark Datasets and Model Configurations
B.1 Benchmark Datasets and Splits
B.1.1 Dataset Curation and Target Processing
Starting from the frozen 51-dataset TabArena panel, we added active OpenML supervised-learning datasets with a
single default target, at least 200 observations, at most 500 registered columns including the target, and a nonsparse
representation. Classification datasets were additionally restricted to 2–10 classes. The sample-size floor supports
repeated 50/50 train–test splits, while the column and class limits keep the additional tasks within the intended operating
range of TabPFNv2. We manually excluded sequential or time-series tasks, survival or time-to-event outcomes, and
other tasks incompatible with an i.i.d. tabular evaluation, and reconciled duplicate or alternative versions of the same
source and prediction target. The resulting benchmark contains 160 classification and 81 regression datasets. Each task
uses its designated default target. We discard observations with missing classification labels or nonfinite regression
responses. Classification labels are deterministically mapped to consecutive integers, and the same mapping is used to
align predicted probabilities with evaluation labels.
B.1.2 Sampling and Train–Test Splits
For each of five repetitions, we sample at most 10,000 observations without replacement and allocate ⌊N/2⌋ to training
and the remainder to testing. Regression observations are sampled uniformly. For classification, we use stratified
sampling with class allocations as close as possible to proportional while preserving every class in the sampled data and
both partitions; observations are sampled uniformly within each class.
Every model uses the same observations and outer partition within a repetition. TFMs receive the entire training
partition as context, giving a maximum context size of 5,000, and BART fits on the same partition. RealMLP and
XGBoost reserve an internal validation subset of the outer training partition as specified in Section B.2.3. Meta-features
use only the outer training partition.

B.2.3 Conventional Baselines
BART.We use the high-level bartz.Bart interface in bartz 0.12.1 and retain its default model, prior, and MCMC
settings. We keep 1,000 post-burn-in draws in total, balanced across the package’s default four chains, to use a fixed
Monte Carlo sample size for every dataset and task.
The remaining choices adapt the interface to heterogeneous tabular inputs and benchmark outputs. We median-impute
numeric features and one-hot encode nominal features because bartz uses ordered threshold splits; unseen categories
yield an all-zero indicator block. To prevent a categorical feature with many levels from receiving greater prior split
probability, we divide each original feature’s prior mass uniformly among its one-hot columns. Binary classification
uses the package’s probit outcome. For multiclass tasks, we fit one binary probit outcome per class and normalize the
probabilities within each posterior draw. Regression uses posterior predictive outcome draws, including observation
noise, so that empirical quantiles describe the predictive rather than latent-mean distribution; the point prediction is the
draw mean.
RealMLP/XGBoost validation and model selection.RealMLP and XGBoost share one 80/20 internal train-
ing/validation partition for each benchmark repetition. Classification validation allocation preserves classes in the fitting
subset and stratifies where feasible; regression uses a random holdout. Numeric missing values are replaced by context
medians (zero for an entirely missing column), and categoricals are ordinal-encoded with a separate missing level and
−1 for unseen categories. RealMLP receives categorical column indicators for its embedding layers; XGBoost treats
the encoded values as numeric features. This outer preprocessing is fitted on the benchmark training partition.
Both fixed and HPO variants select their epoch or boosting round by validation loss. Classification uses cross-entropy,
and regression uses mean pinball loss over all 199 quantiles. To make the nominal search budgets comparable, both
HPO variants evaluate 50 configurations and cap each candidate at 256 epochs or boosting rounds. These limits
standardize the number of candidates and maximum training stages, but do not equate wall-clock time or computational
cost across model families. The best validation checkpoint of the selected configuration supplies the test prediction,
with no full-training refit or ensemble over HPO candidates. Both model families predict the common quantile grid
directly. Their regression point prediction is the integral of the piecewise-linear quantile function over [0,1] , with
constant extension beyond the first and last grid levels.
RealMLP and RealMLP-HPO.We use the RealMLP-TD and RealMLP-HPO estimators provided by PyTabKit 1.7.3.
The fixed variant follows the package’s published TD configuration, while the HPO variant uses PyTabKit’s unmodified
default search space. We specify only the common experimental controls described above. For classification, the
fixed variant disables label smoothing, whereas the HPO space retains the package’s label-smoothing choices. For
regression, both variants replace the standard point-prediction objective with mean pinball loss on the common quantile
grid and integrate the resulting quantile function for point prediction.
XGBoost and XGBoost-HPO.The fixed variant uses XGBoost 3.2.0 with histogram tree construction, otherwise
standard tree defaults, and at most 256 boosting rounds. Classification uses the binary or multiclass logistic objective;
regression uses reg:quantileerror with one output tree per quantile. XGBoost-HPO uses a prespecified search
space based on the TabArena protocol and held fixed across datasets. We raise the learning-rate lower bound from 0.005
to 0.02 to avoid systematic undertraining under the common 256-round cap. Table 5 reports the resulting distributions.

C.4 Aggregation, Ranks, and Uncertainty Intervals
Within each dataset and configuration, we average each metric over its available repetitions. Ranks are then computed
within datasets, with average ranks for ties; accuracy and R2 are ranked in descending order and all losses in ascending
order. Mean ranks give equal weight to each dataset. Joint point/distribution profiles use their common set of models.
Their absolute coordinates, raw calibration profiles, and vanilla coverage–width profiles use the median of the dataset-
level averages; regression CRPS is first normalized by its split-specific training-response standard deviation. Conformal
profiles and paired coverage-error changes use their mean; relative width changes against Vanilla use the median of
dataset-level ratios.
All bootstrap intervals use 10,000 resamples of dataset identifiers with replacement and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.
A resampled dataset carries all its model and method results, preserving pairing. For an error change, we first subtract
the vanilla error on the same repetition, average within datasets, and then bootstrap the dataset means. These are
pointwise 95% intervals, without adjustment for multiple comparisons or grouping by dataset family. They reflect
variation over datasets, not over new training splits. Model differences are assessed using paired differences rather than
overlap between individual intervals. Variation across meta-prediction repetitions is instead reported as one sample
standard deviation, as stated in the corresponding captions.

E.3 Meta-Predictor Configuration and Cross-Validation
Cross-validation design.All meta-predictors use five shuffled outer folds in each of five repetitions, with datasets
as the sampling units. Fold assignments are shared across metrics, sets of models, information groups, and learners.
Within each outer training fold, we estimate bBm and construct the average-shift target qd and relative-deviation targets
hdm. No dataset appears in both training and validation/test data.
Meta-feature preprocessing.For component prediction, nonfinite meta-features are treated as missing, imputed
with training-fold medians, and augmented with missingness indicators. Entirely missing training columns are set to
zero. We then apply a training-fold quantile transform with min(100, Dtrain) landmarks, followed by centering and
unit-variance scaling. The same fitted transformations are applied to validation and test data and refitted within every
inner fold.
Ridge predictor.Ridge includes an intercept and minimizes squared error plus λ∥β∥2
2, with λ∈ {0.1,1,10,100,1000} .
Three shuffled inner folds select separate penalties for predictingqd and hdm. Baseline means and targets are recomputed
within each inner training fold; model-specific outputs receive equal weight, and ties select the smallest penalty.
Extra-Trees predictor.Extra-Trees uses 100 trees, squared-error splits, minimum leaf size four, minimum split size
two, 70% feature subsampling, no bootstrap sampling, and unrestricted depth. A scalar regressor predicts qd, and a
multi-output regressor predicts the model-specific values hdm. We subtract the mean prediction across models from the
predictedh dm values to enforce the zero-sum constraint. Extra-Trees has no hyperparameter search; the expanded set
of models is evaluated separately, while incremental information comparisons use Ridge with the TFMs.

Auxiliary prediction scores.Linear classification uses logistic regression with C= 1 , an L2 penalty, the SAGA solver,

G.1 Experimental Protocol
The conformal experiment uses the seven distributional TFMs and BART, with base-model training and predictions
identical to the vanilla benchmark. From an original test set of size n, a shared random permutation assigns ⌊n/4⌋
observations to an auxiliary block A, the next ⌊n/4⌋ to a calibration block C, and the remainder to a held-out block
T . The allocation is shared across base models, methods, and nominal levels, and base predictions remain frozen
throughout post-processing. Each method receives the same labeled development pool P=A∪C , although the
number of labels used for final calibration and the computational cost need not be identical. Here A is the auxiliary
fitting block, C is the final calibration block, and T is the held-out evaluation block; P is the development pool available
before evaluation. The method-specific allocations are described below. We fit each post-processor separately at
α∈ {0.05,0.10,0.15,0.20}.
Within this common allocation, SCP-ABS, SCP-NORM, SCP-CQR, and SCP-PIT perform global split-conformal
calibration on the pooled development set P ; SCP-CQR uses the conformalized quantile-regression construction of
Romano et al. (2019). CalLCP and RLCP likewise use P for localized calibration, following Guan (2021) and Hore and
Foygel Barber (2023), respectively. SLCP fits its local score model on A and calibrates the resulting score correction on
C (Han et al., 2022). PCP and RCP also use A for auxiliary fitting and C for final calibration, following Zhang and
Candès (2024) and Plassier et al. (2025), respectively.
Evaluation and aggregation.We divide T into search and evaluation halves using the WSC procedure in Appendix C.
All methods share the evaluation observations, while each method selects its own worst slab on the search half. The
comparison retains dataset–repetition pairs for which every method–model combination is present and WSC is defined
at every nominal level. We first average retained repetitions within each dataset and then weight datasets equally.
Uncertainty intervals use paired bootstrap resampling of whole datasets. Width summaries additionally exclude
evaluation blocks with response standard deviation at most 10−10; this exclusion is applied to all configurations on the
same dataset–repetition pair. The main-text Figure 8 compares Vanilla and SCP-CQR at 90% nominal coverage on this
positive-variance subset.

G.2 Post-processing Methods
G.2.1 Global Split Conformal Methods
Let µ(x) be the stored point prediction, Qx the monotone quantile-grid interpolant, and Fx its CDF interpolant. We use
four split-conformal scores, including the signed quantile score of Romano et al. (2019):
sABS(x, y) =|y−µ(x)|, s NORM(x, y) = |y−µ(x)|
max{σ(x),10 −8} ,
sCQR(x, y) = max{Qx(α/2)−y, y−Q x(1−α/2)}, s PIT(x, y) = max{Fx(y),1−F x(y)}.
For NORM, σ2(x) is the quantile-grid second moment minus its squared first moment, floored at 10−16. Both moments
use trapezoidal integration and constant quantile extension to 0 and 1, so every base model uses the same construction.
All four variants use the same finite-sample calibration quantile. With ncal calibration scores, the threshold t is their kth
order statistic, where k=⌈(n cal + 1)(1−α)⌉ ; we set t= +∞ when k > ncal. ABS returns [µ−t, µ+t] , NORM
returns[µ−tσ, µ+tσ], and CQR returns
[Qx(α/2)−t, Q x(1−α/2) +t].
The CQR threshold remains signed, so a negative threshold shrinks the interval. PIT returns [Qx(1−t), Qx(t)] for
t <1 and the real line for t≥1 . Its inversion uses interpolation on 100 equally spaced probabilities from 0.001 to
0.999; CDF evaluation uses the original 199-point grid and the repeated-quantile convention in Appendix C.

encoded with a separate missing category and all-zero blocks for unseen categories. This transformation is fitted on A.

G.2.3 Learned Conditional Adaptation
RCP.Following Plassier et al. (2025), RCP fits a distributional regressor to covariates and signed CQR scores. Its
estimated (1−α) score quantile is bτ(x). On a disjoint calibration block we compute ri =s i −bτ(xi) and obtain its
split-conformal thresholdu. The resulting CQR threshold isbτ(x) +u; no division or nonnegative clipping is applied.
The score regressor uses the same model family, checkpoint, preprocessing, and eight-member inference configuration
as the corresponding base TFM, with the scores replacing the response. The BART score regressor uses the configuration
in Appendix B.2.3. RCP uses A as the score-model context and evaluates the learned score quantile on C and T ;
conformal correction is calibrated on C. Here A is separate post-hoc fitting data, not the base model’s training set. The
original response predictor remains frozen.
PCP.PCP (Zhang and Candès, 2024) is applied to absolute residuals from the frozen predictor, using A for auxiliary
fitting and C for calibration. We retain this score because the published regression theory and the authors’ official
implementation are formulated for the absolute-residual PCP construction. Replacing it with a CQR score would
define a separate PCP–CQR variant, requiring its own score model and prediction-set inversion. We therefore reuse
the authors’ official implementation, available at https://github.com/yaozhang24/pcp, and retain its prescribed
fitting and tuning procedure. Inputs use the auxiliary-fitted imputed, standardized, and one-hot representation defined
above; the official implementation then applies its own standardization and intercept. We fit a fresh PCP instance at
each nominal level.

Infinite intervals.Figure 39 summarizes infinite-interval rates across base models and target coverage levels. From
80% to 95% target coverage, the base-model-averaged rate rises from 14.3% to 44.1% for PCP, from 0.62% to 3.65%
for CalLCP, and from 0.12% to 0.76% for RLCP. All other methods, including SCP-CQR, remain at zero. Because any
positive rate makes the arithmetic mean width infinite, coverage improvements from these methods must be interpreted
together with this loss of informativeness. An infinite interval occurs when conformal inversion remains feasible as
the score threshold tends to +∞; under localized weighting, this can arise when a query has insufficient effective
calibration mass for a finite threshold.

H Complete Ensemble-Size Results
H.1 Evaluation Protocol
We vary the estimator count overE∈ {1,4,8,16,32} while holding the dataset splits and all other model settings fixed.
The classification sweep contains all 11 TFMs on 160 datasets and the regression sweep contains all nine regression
TFMs on 81 datasets, with five seeds per configuration. For each metric, we first average the five seeds within each
dataset and model–ensemble configuration, then take the median metric across the common set of datasets. CRPS is
normalized at the trial level by the population standard deviation of the training response from the same dataset–seed
split before aggregation. Higher values are better for accuracy and R2; lower values are better for all other reported
metrics.
H.2 Additional Results
H.2.1 Median-Metric Results
The median-metric analysis separates predictive metrics from calibration, but also shows that predictive behavior is
metric-dependent. Accuracy and both proper scores generally improve, with most gains occurring at small to moderate
ensemble sizes. Median R2 is mixed across models. Calibration metrics remain less monotonic: the preferred ensemble
size depends on both the model and the metric, so larger ensembles do not uniformly improve calibration.

H.2.2 Coverage–Width Results
The median metric plots summarize marginal and subgroup coverage errors, but do not show the corresponding interval
widths. We therefore complement them with coverage–width summaries for regression intervals. Increasing E changes
the coverage–sharpness operating point: movements toward nominal coverage can be accompanied by wider intervals,
and marginal and worst-slab coverage need not change in the same way. Consequently, a better average coverage value
alone does not establish better uncertainty quality or subgroup robustness.
