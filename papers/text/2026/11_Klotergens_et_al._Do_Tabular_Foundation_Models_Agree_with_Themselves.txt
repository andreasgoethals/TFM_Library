Do Tabular Foundation Models Agree with Themselves?
Christian Kl¨ otergens, Vijaya Krishna Y alavarthi, Lars Schmidt-Thieme, T om Hanika
Institute of Computer Science, University of Hildesheim, Hildesheim, Germany
Abstract
Tabular Foundation Models (TFMs) are the
state of the art for many tabular predic-
tion tasks. They are transformers that
approximate the Bayesian posterior predic-
tive distribution under a pretraining prior.
TFMs become multivariate predictors au-
toregressively, by sampling one target and
appending it to the features. This paper
is the first to evaluate whether this setup
induces a valid stochastic process. Infor-
mally, we ask:Do we get the same answer
to the same question asked in two different
ways?The standard Kolmogorov consis-
tency conditions cannot be checked on TFMs
directly, since TFMs are unable to model
certain conditional distributions. We there-
fore propose two adapted, testable require-
ments. Marginalization consistency demands
that marginalized conditionals equal directly
predicted marginals, and factorization consis-
tency that both factorization orders yield the
same joint. Since the data-generating distri-
bution satisfies both, a perfect TFM would
as well, and every violation lower-bounds
how far the model’s predictions are from the
ground truth. Every TFM we evaluate vio-
lates both requirements, for classification and
regression, on all datasets.

For conditional neural processes [12], such autoregres-
sive constructions [6] are known not to represent a
valid stochastic process. A family of finite-dimensional
distributions defines a stochastic process if and only if
it satisfies the Kolmogorov consistency conditions [28]:
permuting the variables must not change their joint
distribution, and marginalizing a variable out must
recover the distribution of the remaining ones. A
neural process whose predictions factorize over test
points satisfies both conditions, if the context is fixed.

not be checked directly. The conditions marginalize
a target while holding the context fixed, whereas a
TFM cannot be queried with a column that is present

the value as missing is not the same as leaving the col-

all they ask is that predictions of a TFM do not con-
tradict predictions of the same TFM.
Figure 1 shows what an inconsistency looks like us-

the assumptions that the color is either red or white
(which are all possible wine colors in the dataset).
The mixture is weighted by the probability for the
respective color. The true joint distribution over all
columns in the dataset is unknown, yet any single
joint distribution behind both queries would give them
the same marginal distribution ofQuality-Rating.
TabPFNv3, however, computes two very different dis-
tributions, hence it disagrees with itself.
To guide the ongoing research on TFMs towards mini-
mizing or even eliminating such inconsistencies, we for-
mulate two requirements that any model whose predic-
tions are induced by a well-defined joint must satisfy.
First,marginalization consistency, the counterpart of
the marginalization condition, which ties the model’s
conditionals back to its directly predicted marginals.
Second,factorization consistency, the counterpart of
the permutation condition, which demands that the
two chain-rule orders build the same joint distribution.
Unlike the classical conditions, both compare only pre-
dictions that a TFM can make. We show that both
conditions follow from the law of total probability and
the chain rule. Furthermore, we formally show that
these two consistency requirements are closely con-
nected. Factorization consistency implies marginal-
ization consistency, but not vice versa. Hence, every
observed marginalization failure already certifies that
changing the order of factorization will lead to different
distributions. Since the data-generating distribution
satisfies both requirements, every violation also lower-
bounds the distance between the model’s predictions
and the ground truth.
We also design experiments to evaluate whether and to
what extent different TFM classifiers and (probabilis-
tic) regressors violate each consistency requirement.

MODELS
Tabular foundation models transfer the in-context
learning paradigm to supervised prediction on tables.

The standard blueprint is the prior-data fitted net-
work (PFN; Figure 2). A transformer is pretrained
on millions of synthetic datasets drawn from a prior
over data-generating processes (for instance, randomly
sampled structural causal models) by predicting held-
out target values from the remainder of each dataset.
After pretraining, the model solves a new task without
any gradient updates. It consumes a labeled training
setD={(x i, yi)}n i=1 as its context together with a
test feature vectorx, and a single forward pass re-
turns a predictive distribution ˆp(y|x;D) over the tar-
get. The pretraining objective shapes this output into
an approximation of the Bayesian posterior predictive
distribution under the synthetic prior [22].

quantiles [30], depending on the model. We treat clas-
sification and regression in a unified way. A target

3 CONSISTENCY PROPERTIES
We adapt the consistency conditions of stochastic pro-
cesses to the way TFMs are built, and formalize two
requirements that are necessary to guarantee that the
predicted posteriors stem from a single, well-defined
multivariate distribution. We begin with the weaker
of the two, and then show that one implies the other.
Notation for a pair of targets.Both requirements
already apply ford= 2, so we state them for two
targets and writeAandBfor the two target columns,
a∈ Y A andb∈ Y B for their values. Superscripts
indicate target columns throughout:D A is the context
set in which columnAis the target andBis one of the
features, andD A −B is the same context with columnB
removed. Ford >2 both conditions apply verbatim to
any pair of targets, with the remaining ones held fixed
as features.
Marginalization consistency .The first require-
ment ties the model’s conditionals back to its direct
univariate predictions: sampling the intermediate tar-
get from the model’s own predictive distribution must
not change what the modelbelievesabout the remain-
ing target. In the spirit of the Kolmogorov Extension
Theorem [28], marginalizing the factorization must re-
cover the direct marginal.

4 EXPERIMENTS
We want to put the just-introduced conditions to the
test on current TFMs. The goal is to detect and quan-
tify to what extent each condition is violated. The
proposed method shall establish a new aspect for an-
alyzing and comparing TFMs and their prediction ca-
pabilities. Furthermore, we examine the relationship
between the two consistency requirements.
4.1 Setup
Models.We conduct our experiments on several
state-of-the-art TFMs: TabPFNv2/v3 [13, 15], whose
regression head returns a bin-wise piecewise-constant
density, TabICLv2 [30], whose head returns predicted
quantiles, and TabDPTv1.2 [17, 20], whose head is a
softmax over equal-width bins of the standardized tar-
get space. For classification, we additionally include
TabICLv1 [29] and TabFM [18]. While the former
does not provide a regressor, the latter offers point
regression only.
Datasets.We employ tabular datasets from

the remaining features, with all remaining columns

counts, and OpenML ID, together with the selection

4.4 Discussion
Every model violates both conditions on every dataset,
so the observed inconsistency is a structural property
of current TFMs. TabFM, the newest and top-ranked
model on TabArena [8], comes closest to being consis-
tent among all tested classifiers. Based on that, one
could assume that a model’s consistency automatically
improves with its predictive performance. However,
relating each model’s dataset-level accuracy or RMSE
to its consistency gap (Appendix F) reveals no strong
correlation. Therefore, consistency is a distinct axis
that benchmarks such as TabArena do not capture.
We find a strong association between the two consis-
tency types (see Figure 3). Additionally, for the clas-
sification task we find that the TV measures for the
two consistency requirements are higher for datasets
with fewer features. However, we do not observe this
for regression (see Appendix G).
TabDPT is a revealing case in two aspects. First, it
is trained with a self-supervised objective that pre-
dicts each column from the others [17, 20]. As the
roles of target and feature are repeatedly swapped dur-
ing training, one would intuitively expect it to align
the two conditional directions and thus improve fac-
torization consistency. This expectation is not met:
TabDPT is one of the more inconsistent models un-
der factorization, while it is relatively consistent un-
der marginalization. Second, unlike the other mod-
els, TabDPT is trained not on synthetic but on real
data, a corpus that includes those standard OpenML
tables in our suite that are not drawn from the curated
TabArena re-uploads. Yet it is not measurably more
consistent on datasets seen during training than on the
curated ones it has not seen. TabArena datasets are

5 RELATED WORK
Coherence of predictive systems.That a sequence
of one-step-ahead predictions defines a valid joint is the
defining property of a coherent (martingale) predic-
tive system. This is the foundation of the martingale-
posterior view of Bayesian inference [4, 11]. Falck et al.
[9] use exactly this martingale property to test whether
in-context learning in large language models (LLMs)
is Bayesian. TabMGP builds martingale posteriors on
top of TabPFN, implicitly assuming the requisite co-
herence [25].
Our marginalization-consistency condition is a tabu-
lar, across-column analog of the martingale property
these works rely on, adapted to the way TFMs are
queried (Appendix A). Its violation therefore does not
refute the martingale property itself, but it shows that
TFMs do not deliver coherence for free, so construc-
tions that assume it need to verify it.
Incoherence of any-order models.The tension
between univariate training and joint deployment is
well documented outside the tabular setting. Order-
agnostic autoregressive models train a single network
to model every conditional ordering [16, 33], but the
orderings do not necessarily agree. Shih et al. [32] show
these models are internally redundant, defining the
same distribution in several inconsistent ways. LLMs
likewise return probability judgments that violate the
basic properties of probability [19, 37]. We bring this
lens to tabular foundation models, and unlike these
qualitative diagnoses, we derive metrics that quantify
the extent of inconsistency.
Kolmogorov consistency in neural processes.
Neural processes are meta-learned predictors that map
a context set to a predictive distribution, and PFNs
are an instance of them [23]. It is known that they

generally fail the Kolmogorov consistency conditions
that are required of stochastic processes [28]. Young
[36] precisely defines this for conditional neural pro-
cesses [12], establishing aconditioning consistency gap
between adding a point to the context and condition-
ing on it. Bruinsma et al. [6] deploy conditional neu-
ral processes autoregressively, feeding sampled targets
back into the context, which is how TFMs build multi-
variate joints. They point out that this gives up consis-
tency under permutation and marginalization, study
how the ordering of the target points affects the pre-
dictive log-likelihood, and recommend averaging over
random orderings. However, they do not measure the
discrepancy between the joints themselves. Mortimer
et al. [21] measure how causal masking makes the pre-
dictions of transformer neural processes depend on the
order in which observed points are streamed into the
context, as a KL gap to the average over orderings.
Our conditions are the tabular counterpart of these
gaps, with two differences. First, the Kolmogorov con-
ditions for neural processes fix the context, which a
TFM cannot do, since it cannot predict a marginal
from a context that contains the marginalized column;
our conditions compare predictions from the contexts
a TFM can use (Appendix A). Second, TFMs are de-
ployed with the finite, and often small, context sizes
at which such gaps are largest, and we measure the
violation directly, as a distance between distributions,
rather than bounding it asymptotically or through its
effect on the log-likelihood.
Yalavarthi et al. [35] define marginalization consis-
tency in the context of probabilistic forecasting for ir-
regularly sampled multivariate time series. They show
experimentally that a previous model fails to fulfill
that condition, and introduce a consistent one.
Compatibility of conditionals.Deciding whether
a family of conditional and marginal distributions can
arise from a common joint is a fundamental problem
in statistics [2, 3, 5]. The same problem underlies de-
pendency networks, where independently fitted condi-
tionals are combined without any guarantee of coher-
ence [14]. Copulas offer the complementary, coherent-
by-construction route to a joint [24] by separating the
marginals from the dependence structure. Our condi-
tions reflect the compatibility requirements from the
literature, and are turned into tests on a TFM’s pre-
dictions.
6 CONCLUSION
TFMs become multivariate predictors by chaining uni-
variate conditionals, a construction known not to yield
a valid stochastic process for conditional neural pro-
cesses. We asked whether this construction yields
a valid stochastic process for TFMs, or informally,
whether a TFM gives the same answer to the same
question asked in two different ways. Since a TFM
cannot be queried with a column in the context but
not at the test point, we adapted the Kolmogorov
conditions into marginalization and factorization con-
sistency, which compare only predictions a TFM can
make. As the data-generating distribution satisfies
both requirements, every violation lower-bounds the
model’s distance to the ground truth. Every TFM we
evaluate violates both requirements, on every dataset,
for classification and regression. The answer to our
question is therefore negative: TFMs give different an-
swers to the same question asked in two different ways.
TabFM, which has the highest Elo on TabArena [8]
among the evaluated models, comes closest to con-
sistency among the classifiers. However, in general,
we did not find that accuracy (Appendix F) leads to
consistency. What we did find in our experiments is
that the measures for marginalization and factoriza-
tion consistency do correlate with each other.
Our results have an impact beyond the joint distribu-
tions of autoregressive constructions. For example, the
fact that TFMs are not marginalization-consistent has
practical implications for the handling of missing val-
ues. Imputing the missing value ((C1)’s RHS) yields a
different prediction than dropping the respective col-
umn from the context ((C1)’s LHS).
For future work, one could investigate different strate-
gies that yield TFMs that adhere to our consistency
requirements. One possible direction is to penalize
consistency violations during pretraining on the prior.
Alternatively, one could try to construct TFM archi-
tectures that are restricted to predicting joint distri-
butions in a way that induces a consistent stochastic
process. While current TFMs only predict univariate
distributions, future TFM versions could be equipped
with a multivariate head. This would open up novel
questions on the consistency of TFMs, relating to per-
mutation invariance and marginalization consistency
of the now multivariate target distributions. However,
our conditions remain relevant, as a multivariate TFM
can also be chained autoregressively, and the autore-
gressive joints must match the predicted joint. In gen-
eral, finding out whether consistency can be achieved
without sacrificing expressivity is an intriguing open
question for the ongoing research on TFMs.

A FROM STOCHASTIC PROCESSES TO TFMS
This appendix relates conditions (C1) and (C2) to the consistency conditions of stochastic processes. We state
the classical conditions and explain why a TFM cannot be queried for them (Section A.1) and derive both

A.1 The Classical Condition and the TFM Interface
A stochastic process is a family of finite-dimensional distributions that is consistent under marginalization: the
distribution of any set of variables is the marginal of the distribution of every larger set [28]. Neural processes [12]
carry this requirement over to predictions. For a fixed context, the prediction for a set of targets must be the
marginal of the prediction for every larger set of targets. Transposed to the columns of a table, the context is
the training setDwith all of its columns, the targets are the test valuesaandb, and the condition reads

A.4 The Data-Generating Distribution
Our conditions are meant to hold for the target of learning, the data-generating distributionp ⋆(a, b|x). Used
as a predictor,p ⋆ answers each TFM query with the distribution of the target given the columns that the query
contains. Removing a column from the table amounts to marginalizing it out ofp ⋆: if the rows of a table are
drawn fromp ⋆, the same table without columnBhas rows drawn from its marginal over (x, a). The answer to
the direct query ˆp(a|x;D A −B) is thereforep ⋆(a|x) = R p⋆(a|b,x)p ⋆(b|x) db, and the answer to the conditional
query isp ⋆(a|b,x). Neither depends on the training values in the context, so (5) holds trivially, and all four
heads are the conditionals and marginals of the single jointp ⋆. Condition (C1) is then its law of total probability
and (C2) its chain rule, so both hold exactly.
Forp ⋆, the difference between (3) and (C1) thus disappears: removing the column of an unobserved target and
integrating that target out give the same distribution. A TFM that has learnedp ⋆ satisfies both of our conditions
whatever context it is given, and every violation is a departure fromp ⋆, whose size Proposition 3 bounds from
below.

D DISCRETIZATION OF THE REGRESSION CHECKS
Section 4 states that the regression checks compare distributions on a shared 20-cell grid (marginalization) and
a shared 128×128 grid (factorization). This appendix gives the exact construction of those cells and, since the
models expose their predictive distributions in three different parameterizations, how each head is reduced to
the single interface the checks consume. Every step below is a deterministic function of the fitted models: there
is no sampling anywhere in the pipeline, so repeating a run reproduces the reported numbers exactly.
Common interface: the quantile curve.The checks never touch a model’s internals. Each head is reduced
to aquantile curve: its inverse CDF ˆF −1 evaluated on one fixed ladder ofL= 2,000 equally spaced levels running
fromα 1 = 2.5×10 −4 toα L = 0.99975. Consecutive levels therefore bracket 5×10 −4 of predicted mass, and at
most 2.5×10 −4 is left beyond the ladder in each tail. A head may return a slightly non-monotone level sequence
(quantile crossing); we take a running maximum along the ladder, which restores a valid inverse CDF and acts
as the identity whenever the head is already monotone. Everything downstream is a function of the pairs (α ℓ, qℓ)
alone, so all models enter through the same interface and no model-specific tolerance or tuning is involved.
Per-model access to ˆF −1.The heads parameterize the predictive distribution differently, and each is inverted
in the way its own parameterization prescribes.
•T abPFNv2 and v3emit a full-support bar distribution: a softmax over 5,000 bars, piecewise-constant
within a bar, with half-normal tails attached to the outermost two. Quantiles come from the model’s own
inversion of that CDF. Because the density is available in closed form, it is also what the reported NLL uses
directly, with no finite-difference approximation.
•T abICLv2emits a quantile distribution: 999 predicted knots at levelsk/1,000 fork= 1, . . . ,999, joined by
a monotone spline in the interior and extended by generalized-Pareto tails beyond the outermost knots. Its
quantile function is the head’s native output, so evaluating the ladder is a direct call; its density is recovered
from the reciprocal slope of the quantile curve.
•T abDPTemits a softmax over 2,048 equal-width bins spanning a fixed interval of thestandardizedtarget
space. Its public predictor returns only the bin-center expectation, which is a point prediction and cannot
support a distributional check; we therefore read the bin weights off the regression head itself and invert the
resulting piecewise-linear CDF, mapping the bin edges back to raw target units with the same standardization
the head applies to its training targets. Its density, like TabPFN’s, is exact.
A head’s native resolution never binds the comparison: the coarsest of the three, TabICLv2’s 999 knots, is
already some fifty times finer than the 20 cells on which TV is finally evaluated.

Two properties of this construction matter for reading the numbers. The grid isshared: both sides are discretized
on the same edges with the same folding rule, so the boundary treatment is common to the two sides and
cannot by itself open a gap. And the grid ismodel-specific: because its endpoints are read off the model’s own
predicted support, a cell has a different width for different models, datasets and instances. This does not impair
comparability, because TV always compares two distributions produced by the same model on the same grid
and is unit-free.
The128×128cells (factorization).Here the two axes are discretized separately, and each axis is spanned
by its own marginal head alone: the 129a-edges are equally spaced across [q a 1 , qa L], theα 1- andα L-quantiles of

grid, since each is evaluated only to fill one line of the joint table.

The quantile ladder, the atoms and the grid each approximate, so even a perfectly consistent model could score
TV>0 under the construction of Appendix D. We measure this floor by running the unmodified checks on a
model that is consistent by construction: Gaussian process regression [31] with two outputs (a, b) over inputs
x∈[0,1] 2, coupled by an intrinsic coregionalization kernel [1],

rows drawn from its own prior and queried at 20 test inputs. Every head the checks request is a GP prediction
from this context. The marginals ˆp(a|x) and ˆp(b|x) are the predictive distributions atx, and the conditional
ˆp(a|b,x) is the predictive distribution ofaonce the observationbatxhas been added to the context (and
symmetrically for ˆp(b|a,x)). All four heads are conditionals of one Gaussian process, so both consistency
conditions hold exactly and any nonzero TV is discretization error. The GP also satisfies (5): the process

Shifting or rescaling either target moves the grid, the atoms and the cell representatives along with it, so the TV

TV<10 −12, which is zero up to floating-point error. Forρ∈ {0.5,0.9,0.99}at the resolutions behind Table 1,
the floor is at most 4.5×10 −4 for marginalization and 1.5×10 −3 for factorization. That is more than 35 and 90
times smaller, respectively, than the smallest mean gap of any TFM (0.0163 and 0.1307), so the TFM gaps are
not artifacts of the discretization. Refining every resolution jointly by a factorc(K= 1,000catoms, 128c×128c
factorization cells and a ladder ofL= 2,000clevels, with the 20 marginalization cells held fixed) drives the floor
towards zero: the marginalization floor halves with every doubling ofcand reaches 5.3×10 −5 atc= 8, while the
factorization floor falls to 2.7×10 −4. A consistent model thus attains TV = 0 in the limit, and at the paper’s
resolution it comes within 1.5×10 −3 of it. The floor has two sources. The ladder spreads the mass of each
quantile bin uniformly across the bin, which misplaces mass in the tails, where the bins are wide. The atoms
(marginalization) or cell representatives (factorization) approximate the integral overb, and this error grows
withρ, because a sharper conditional changes faster across a cell. It dominates on the coarse factorization grids
(c <1), where the floor accordingly rises withρ.

F PREDICTIVE QUALITY VERSUS CONSISTENCY
Figures 6 and 7 plot each model’s classification accuracy against its factorization and marginalization TV gap,
one panel per dataset; Figures 8 and 9 do the same for regression, plotting RMSE in place of accuracy. Across
all four figures, more accurate (or lower-RMSE) models are not systematically more consistent within a dataset:
the two quantities do not trade off, but neither does either one predict the other from a single panel.
The dataset-level view above averages over instances, which leaves open how the inconsistency is distributed over
rows. Figure 5 resolves this at the level of single test rows. For each instance we pair the negative log-likelihood
that the direct predictive ˆp(a|x;D A −B) assigns to the true label with the TV distance between that predictive
and the marginalized mixture on the right-hand side of (C1).
Instances whose NLL is near zero are those on which the direct head is already close to a point mass on one
class. Mixing overbcannot move a saturated predictive far, so the TV on such rows is bounded near zero for
arithmetic reasons alone, independently of whether the model is coherent. Keeping them would draw a diagonal
band into the bottom-left corner of every panel and manufacture the appearance of TV growing steadily with the
loss, an artifact of saturation rather than evidence that hard instances are the inconsistent ones. We therefore
drop every instance with an NLL below 0.05, i.e., every instance on which the model already assigns roughly
95% or more to the correct class, and plot only the remainder.
Figure 5 shows that there is no correlation between the likelihood assigned to the correct label and marginalization
consistency at the per-row level.
