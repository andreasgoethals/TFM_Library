ABSTRACT
Tabular foundation models (TFMs) now outperform gradient-boosted trees in clas-
sification and regression. Yet in survival analysis, which predicts when an event
occurs from censored data, often on small clinical cohorts, we find that no existing
TFM beats the half-century-old Cox model. We address this issue by introducing
SurvICL, a TFM that predicts survival curves in-context. SurvICL is pretrained on
purely synthetic datasets generated from a novel flexible proportional-odds prior
that outperforms other prior types in our ablation study. Unlike most other survival
models, SurvICL is trained with a plain quantile regression loss, which is possi-
ble because its synthetic pre-training data generator can provide uncensored event
times. Additionally, we introduce the largest survival benchmark to date spanning
103 datasets. On the 63 datasets held out from all design decisions, SurvICL out-
performs 20 tree-based, deep, and foundation-model baselines without any tuning:
it wins 82.9% of pairwise comparisons in integrated Brier score, against 71.3% for
the runner-up, the Cox model, and is the first model to rank above Cox on small
datasets.
1 INTRODUCTION
Many decisions hinge onwhenan event will happen: how long will a patient survive after a cancer
diagnosis? When will a customer churn? When will a machine fail? Survival analysis answers such
questions by predicting, for an individual with featuresx∈R d, thesurvival functionS(t|x) =
P(T ⋆ > t|x), that is, the probability that the event has not occurred by timet(Kleinbaum and
Klein, 2012; Machin et al., 2006; Wiegrebe et al., 2024; Jeanselme et al., 2024). What sets survival
analysis apart from regression, aside from strictly positive predictions, iscensoring. A patient who
leaves a clinical study alive after three years does not reveal their time of death, only that it exceeds
three years. Discarding such partial observations biases predictions, and treating them as observed
events is plainly wrong: a survival model must learn from both observed and censored events. In
addition, survival predictions feed decisions (such as choosing a treatment given a five-year survival
probability) and must therefore becalibratedprobabilities rather than mere rankings of individuals
(Goldstein et al., 2020; Lillelund et al., 2025; Alberge et al., 2026).
Survival analysis is often a small-data problem. Many time-to-event datasets come from clinical
studies, where observing events requires following patients for years, and cohorts of a few hundred
individuals are the norm (Indrayan and Mishra, 2021; O’Donnell et al., 2025): in all the data we
collect for this work, more than 70% of the datasets have fewer than 1,000 samples. In this regime,
the flexibility of modern machine learning is a liability. Half a century after its introduction, the
Cox proportional hazards model (Cox, 1972) remains the workhorse of practitioners, and for good
reason: on the small datasets of our benchmark, none of the gradient-boosted trees, deep survival
networks, or existing survival foundation models outperforms it.

Tabular foundation models (TFMs) seem very well suited to this regime. Pre-trained on many syn-
thetic datasets, models such as TabPFN and TabICL predict in-context and now outperform gradient-
boosted trees for classification and regression, in particular on small datasets (Hollmann et al., 2023;
2025; Grinsztajn et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025; 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). This recipe has been ex-
tended beyond standard supervised learning to causal effect estimation (Robertson et al., 2025; Bal-
azadeh et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025b). Survival analysis has not yet benefited from this progress.
Recent survival TFMs either recast survival as classification over a time grid for off-the-shelf TFMs
(Kim et al., 2026), or are pre-trained on synthetic survival data (Seletkov et al., 2026; B ¨ohm et al.,
2026; Qi et al., 2026) but do not beat the Cox model or random survival forests (Figure 1).
Building a High-Performing TFM for Survival Analysis.We introduce SurvICL, a tabular foun-
dation model for in-context survival analysis, built on the TabICLv2 backbone (Qu et al., 2026).
Similar to SurvivalPFN (Qi et al., 2026), it handles censoring in the data generation rather than the
loss. During pre-training, SurvICL sees censored datasets in its context, but is supervised with the
latent, uncensored event times of the simulator: it learns to correct for censoring in-context, so that
a plain quantile-regression loss suffices. SurvICL predicts for each individual a continuous quantile
function of the event time, from which we can directly infer a monotone survival function.
Scaling Evaluation of Survival Models.We evaluate SurvICL in the largest benchmark to date for
survival analysis: 103 real-world datasets from healthcare, genomics, economics and engineering,
with up to 52,000 samples and 15,000 features, against 20 baselines spanning classical, tree-based,
deep and foundation models. For comparison, survival papers at machine-learning venues typically
report results on 5 to 20 datasets, and the largest evaluations of survival foundation models span 48
to 81 datasets (Kim et al., 2026; Lyu et al., 2026; Pham et al., 2026a; Qi et al., 2026). All design
choices were made on 40 development datasets, and all results are reported on 63 held-out datasets
never used during development. On these held-out datasets, SurvICL is the best method both in
prediction accuracy and in discrimination (Figure 1): it wins 82.9% of pairwise comparisons for
the integrated Brier score (IBS), against 71.3% for the runner-up, the Cox model, and in 77.7% for
the C-index, against 71.6% for random survival forests (see Figure 12). On small datasets, where
no previous method surpasses the Cox model, SurvICL is the first to rank above it. On the largest
datasets, gradient boosting still maintains a slight edge. Importantly, these gains do not come at the
expense of computational efficiency. Across predictive metrics and dataset-size regimes, SurvICL
lies on or close to the performance-runtime Pareto frontier (Appendix D). Thus, SurvICL combines
strong predictive performance with efficient out-of-the-box inference. We commit to releasing code
and model in an open-source license.

2 RELATEDWORK
Deep Learning for Survival Analysis.The toolbox of survival analysis has been considerably
extended in the last years to include deep architectures (Katzman et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2018;
Kvamme et al., 2019) that can accommodate a wide variety of data and event types, including time
series, competing events, images and text (Zhu et al., 2016; Thorsen-Meyer et al., 2022; Bleistein
et al., 2024; Wiegrebe et al., 2024; Alberge et al., 2024; Jeanselme et al., 2024). Our work builds
on this research by tailoring deep transformers and training procedures for tabular data, which is the
most common form of survival data.
Deep Learning for Tabular Data.Gradient-based decision tree models have long dominated
benchmarks on tabular data (Gorishniy et al., 2021; Grinsztajn et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2024). Tabular
foundation models have recently challenged their superiority and sparked a new line of research that
focused on designing tailored architectures and training procedures (Hollmann et al., 2023; 2025;
Qu et al., 2025; 2026; Kim et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). We build on these ar-
chitectural advances and adapt them to calibrated survival prediction. Once pre-trained, our method
performs in-context inference without fine-tuning or weight updates.
Tabular Foundation Models for Survival Analysisis a burgeoning field of research. Efforts follow
three distinct strategies.(i) Reducing survival to a task that generic TFMs already solve.Kim et al.
(2026) frame survival prediction as a series of binary classification problems over a time grid, so that
any TFM that handles classification can be deployed for survival analysis. Vieyra (2026) instead
plugs an off-the-shelf TFM regressor into a log-normal accelerated failure time model and impute
censored times iteratively, while SurvFM (Lyu et al., 2026) converts censored follow-up into pseudo-
observations of the restricted mean survival time (RMST), which are then regressed in-context by
unmodified TFMs. These approaches inherit the strengths of generic TFMs, but their backbone has
never seen censored data: they either depend on a time discretization, impose a parametric form
on the event-time distribution, or only output a summary statistic (the RMST) rather than a full
survival function.(ii) Adapting generic TFMs with a survival head.Pham et al. (2026b;a) train
classical survival heads (Cox, DeepHit, multi-task logistic regression) on top of frozen or fine-tuned
representations of TabPFN, TabDPT (Ma et al., 2025a), and TabICL, and systematically compare
these adaptation interfaces on 74 datasets. They find that zero-shot reformulations work best on
small datasets, whereas trained heads take over as the sample size grows. This line of work matches
the common practice of fitting shallow survival models on top of deep foundation models, and gives
up the pre-training + in-context learning recipe: the head must be fitted, and possibly tuned, on each
new dataset.(iii) Pre-training on synthetic survival data.Closest to our work, Seletkov et al. (2026),
B¨ohm et al. (2026) and Qi et al. (2026) pre-train a tabular foundation model on synthetic survival
data, following previous work in the field of tabular foundation models (Hollmann et al., 2023; Qu
et al., 2025; 2026). All three models predict on a discretized time grid (see Table 1), while SurvICL
outputs a continuous quantile function of the event time. Finally, foundation models for time-to-
event prediction have also been pre-trained on large real-world electronic health records (Steinberg
et al., 2024); such models are tied to a specific data modality and coding system, whereas we target
generic tabular data through a synthetic prior.

3.2 DESIGN GOALS FOR ASURVIVALTFM
Performance on Small Datasets.Small datasets are ubiquitous in survival analysis (O’Donnell
et al., 2025), notably because time-to-event data is expensive and time-consuming to collect (to
analyze event times that span overNyears, one first needs to wait forNyears) and because datasets
often originate from healthcare (Indrayan and Mishra, 2021). State-of-the-art models perform well
on large samples (n≥2000), but struggle on small datasets (Zhao et al., 2026).
Out-of-the-Box Calibration.In survival analysis, accurate probability estimates are crucial for
decision-making (Goldstein et al., 2020; Alberge et al., 2026). Yet, models are often trained and
evaluated chasing the C-index, which has been criticized as it does not assess calibration and is not
a proper metric (not minimized for true probabilities, Lillelund et al., 2025). We train SurvICL to
provide calibrated predictions without hyperparameter tuning.

Generating Censoring Times.We add two sources of censoring to our model. First, in50%of
datasets, we apply a shared end-of-study cutoff to a randomly sampled set of individuals. Secondly,
we generate a concurrent censoring time distribution by sampling another pair of baseline and indi-
vidual specific hazards and combining them as for the event time distribution. We then sample from
this second distribution, and set the observed time to be the minimum between the two. Lastly, to
introduce tied times, we move some individual times to a common time grid with low probability.
Filtering.We remove datasets where more than 95% of samples are censored, as well asnon-
predictabledatasets, adapting the filter of Qu et al. (2026): a dataset is non-predictable if predicting
the mean survival time achieves a lower IBS (Section 6) than an ExtraTrees-based survival model
(Geurts et al., 2006). Every pretraining dataset thus carries exploitable signal.

6.2 BASELINES: FROMCOX TOFOUNDATIONMODELS
All models are described in more detail in Appendix B.1, we refer to Table 3 for a synthetic overview.
Shallow models.We include Cox model (Cox, 1972) and KM estimator (Kaplan and Meier, 1958)
as baselines. The latter does not use covariates, and hence any model is expected to outperform it.
Deep Networks.We include five deep networks tailored for survival analysis (Lee et al., 2018;
Katzman et al., 2018; Nagpal et al., 2021; Rindt et al., 2022; Kirpichenko et al., 2026). These
models are fitted on each dataset and use various modeling techniques (from Bayesian prior to a
MLP that predicts the survival function at different times).
Tree-Based Models.Following the seminal work of Ishwaran et al. (2008) on random survival
forests (RSF) and Grinsztajn et al. (2022), a body of work has adapted tree-based methods to survival
analysis. On top of RSF, we evaluate SurvivalBoost (Alberge et al., 2024) and alternative gradient-
boosted tree-based methods implemented insksurv(P ¨olsterl, 2020).
Tabular Foundation Models (TFMs).We include four open-weight tabular foundation models in
our benchmark: SurvPFN (B ¨ohm et al., 2026), SurvivalPFN (Qi et al., 2026), Survival-IC (SIC)
(Seletkov et al., 2026) and TFM4Survival (Kim et al., 2026).
6.3 DATASETS ANDHOLD-OUTPROTOCOL
In total, we consider 103 datasets with varying properties, see Figure 3. The datasets are partitioned
into 40 development datasets used during model development and 63 held-out datasets at random.
For development, we source 2 PyCox (Kvamme et al., 2019), 25 SurvSet (Drysdale, 2022), one from
a challenge and 12 cBioPortal (Cerami et al., 2012; Gao et al., 2013; De Bruijn et al., 2023) datasets.
For held-out datasets, we use 6 from Pycox, 39 from SurvSet and 18 datasets from cBioPortal.
Protocol.All design choices of SurvICL (prior, target, and checkpoint) were made using the 40
development datasets; the 63 held-out datasets were kept for the final evaluation. We evaluated
each method over ten event-stratified 80/20 train–test splits (test sets capped at 4,096 observations).

6.4 RESULTS: SURVICL ISBESTOVERALL
SurvICL is the best survival model overall.On the 63 held-out datasets, SurvICL wins 82.9% of
pairwise comparisons in IBS, against 71.3% for the runner-up, the Cox model (Figure 1), and the dif-
ference is statistically significant (Figure 4a, ranks and winrates are related by an affine transform).
It also achieves the highest win rate in C-index, 77.7% against 71.6% for random survival forests
(Figures 4b and 12). All existing survival TFMs trail behind classical methods. That the runners-up
are the Cox model and random survival forests, with few parameters and strong inductive biases,
underlines how hard survival analysis is for flexible models, which overfit small censored datasets.
SurvICL is the first model to rank above Cox on small datasets.69.8% of our held-out datasets
have fewer than 1,000 samples, making this regime central and not a niche. SurvICL is the first to
rank above Cox in IBS (win rate 84.9% against 77.2%, Figure 23) and it matches Cox in C-index,
although these differences are not statistically significant (Figures 22 and 26).
On large datasets, boosting remains strongest.On held-out datasets with more than 1,000 sam-
ples, SurvivalBoost is clearly the best method in IBS (Figure 37, 86.9% winrate). SurvICL ranks
second in average IBS and first in C-index 50th quantile, ahead of TabSurv-WSA, RSF, and all other
TFMs, although it wins fewer pairwise comparisons than on small datasets (Figure 38).

7 DISCUSSION ANDCONCLUSION
Designing Efficient Survival TFMs.SurvICL differs from existing survival TFMs along three axes
(Table 1).Output and supervision.Unlike previous survival TFMs that rely on discrete time bins
such as TFM4Survival (Kim et al., 2026) and SurvivalPFN (Qi et al., 2026), which use classification
or likelihood losses over time bins, SurvPFN (B ¨ohm et al., 2026) uses a censored likelihood with
a ranking term over 1,000 bins, and Survival-IC (Seletkov et al., 2026) uses the DeepHit loss (Lee
et al., 2018) over 10 bins. SurvICL is the first to output a continuous quantile function. Quantile
outputs are not new per se: TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026) uses a pinball loss for regression, and
censored quantile regression (Portnoy, 2003; Pearce et al., 2022) modifies this loss to account for
censoring. With latent-time supervision, no such modification is needed, as censoring is handled by
the prior. SurvivalPFN also supervises with latent times, but over a time grid and with censoring
times as an additional target, which our ablations show to be detrimental.Prior.SurvivalPFN draws
times from random noised neural networks with several censoring mechanisms, SurvPFN draws
Weibull times from a risk score and a dataset-specific shape, and Survival-IC mixes an extended
hazard and a discrete-time model, with covariate-independent and administrative censoring. Our
PO prior instead combines a flexible, multi-modal baseline hazard with a random risk function, for
both event and censoring times; in our ablations, it outperforms the SurvPFN prior as well as Cox
and AFT priors.Backbone and evaluation.Like Survival-IC, built on TabICL (Qu et al., 2025), we
reuse an existing architecture, TabICLv2, which is not our contribution. Finally, we provide, to our
knowledge, the first comparison of all four survival TFMs, on 63 datasets.
Implications.On the small cohorts that dominate clinical research, SurvICL is the first model
to rank above the Cox model, the default of practitioners for half a century, without any training
or tuning, while remaining among the best-calibrated methods. This makes foundation models a
credible default for survival analysis.

Limitations and outlook.On the largest datasets, gradient boosting remains slightly stronger, call-
ing for longer contexts or light fine-tuning. Baseline hyperparameters are tuned on the first split
and reused across splits; as tuning data then overlaps other splits’ test sets, this protocol, if any-
thing, favors the baselines. SurvICL addresses right-censored, single-event data; competing risks
and time-varying covariates are natural extensions of the prior. We release our code and models to
foster research on foundation models for time-to-event data.

A.1 ARCHITECTURE
We build SurvICL on top of TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026), which is at the time of writing the leading
fully open-source model on the TabArena benchmark (Erickson et al., 2025). While refer to the
original paper for the details, the key components of the TabICLv2 architecture can be summarized
as follows:
•Preprocessing: First, some light preprocessing is applied, including ordinal encoding of
categorical columns. After the preprocessing, the model receives a fully numerical table.
•Target-aware embedding: The original covariates(x ij)∈R n×m are transformed into
embeddings(z ij)∈R n×m×128 containing one embedding vectorz ij ∈R 128 per cell
(i, j). Each embedding vector is obtained from an affine transformation applied to three
cells in the same row (“repeated feature grouping”). An affine transformation of the target
yi is added for the context set rows.
•Column-wise embedding: Each columnjof the resulting embeddings is processed in-
dependently using three induced self-attention blocks that allow interactions between dif-
ferent rows in the same column, resulting in a complexity ofO(nm), which is linear in
the number of rows, unlike a full Transformer. Crucially, the attention is designed to only
retrieve information from the training set, meaning that predictions on test samples are
independent of the other test samples.
•Row-wise interaction: Next, three full transformer blocks allow different columns within
a row to interact. Before these blocks, four extra columns with fixed values are appended,
and after the blocks, the values of these four columns are concatenated and serve as row
embeddings, while the other columns are discarded.
•ICL transformer: After adding another embedding of the targets, the embeddings of each
row interact via 12 regular transformer layers, again making sure that test samples cannot
access other test samples.
•Head: Finally, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) head transforms the output embeddings into
999 quantile predictions (for regression).
Overall, the TabICLv2 architecture has a runtime complexity ofO(n 2 +nm 2), and SurvICL pre-
serves this complexity since it only modifies theO(nm)preprocessing and embedding parts. In
addition, like other TFMs, we use ensembling, where the predictions of eight forward passes with
different preprocessings and column permutations are averaged.
Building on TabICLv2, we introduce the following adaptations to handle survival data:
•Time normalization: We normalize observed times by their mean over the context set,
yielding ˜ti =t i/¯t, where ¯tcontext is the mean duration of the observed times. This makes
the time representation invariant to the choice of measurement units. During pre-training,
both context and query sets are normalized and the mean¯tis taken across context and query
set. During inference (after pre-training), the mean for ¯tis taken across the context set, and
the predicted quantiles are transformed back to the original time scale.
•Censoring-aware target embedding: We replace the scalar target embedding with a joint
representation of the observed time and event indicator. Specifically, we sum the embed-
ding of the continuous time ˜ti and a learned embedding of the binary indicatorδ i. This
representation is added to every cell embedding in context rowi, allowing the column-wise
encoder to distinguish observed events from censored observations.
•Survival information at the ICL stage: We also encode( ˜ti, δi)using a separate pair
of time and event-indicator embeddings whose sum matches the dimension of the row
representation. This embedding is added to context row representations before the ICL
transformer. At both embedding stages, survival outcomes are supplied only for context
rows.
•Survival prediction: The MLP head predicts quantiles of the conditional event-time dis-
tribution, using a softplus transformation to ensure that the quantiles are positive. After

A.2 PRETRAINING
We follow many of the pretraining settings from TabICLv2 but make the following modifications:
• We omit the second and third stages of pretraining, which scale to larger datasets, since the
second stage did not show improvements on our benchmark.
• For computational reasons, we only train for 200K steps instead of 500K. Moreover, our
pretraining consisted of two pretrainings for 100K steps each, meaning that the learning-
rate schedule consisted of two 100K-steps schedules.
• We generate pretraining datasets with 1024 samples each, of which a random fraction in
[0.03,0.9]is used as context (train) and the rest as query (test) samples. We lowered the
lower bound from0.3to0.03to cover the smaller datasets found in our benchmark.
• We pretrain fully in 32-bit floats to avoid potential instabilities.

B.1.3 DEEPNEURALNETWORKS
These methods leverage multi-layer architectures to capture complex, non-linear relationships be-
tween covariates and survival times, often dropping the proportional hazards assumption.
DeepHit (Lee et al., 2018):A discrete-time survival network that optimizes a loss that combines a
likelihood and a ranking metric over an MLP, usable in survival analysis and competing events.
DeepSurv (Katzman et al., 2018):It uses a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to replace the linear
combination of covariates in the standard Cox model, allowing it to learn complex, non-linear inter-
actions while still optimizing the Cox partial likelihood.
DSM (Deep Survival Machines) (Nagpal et al., 2021):DSM uses a neural network to model
the survival function as a mixture of parametric distributions (such as Weibull or Log-Normal),
providing well-calibrated survival estimates.
SumoNet (Rindt et al., 2022):SurvivalMonotonic-net utilizes a partially monotonic neural network
to directly optimize the right-censored log-likelihood.
TabSurv(Kirpichenko et al., 2026): A specialized deep learning framework that adapts modern
tabular neural networks to time-to-event prediction. Because this framework supports multiple
representation backbones, we evaluate several architectural variants that appear throughout our re-
sults: the defaultTabSurv,TabSurv-MLP(using a standard Multi-Layer Perceptron),TabSurv-
RealMLP(utilizing the more robust RealMLP tabular backbone), as well asTabSurv-W ASand
TabSurv-WSA(which incorporate specialized attention and sparse embedding mechanisms de-
signed specifically for tabular features).
B.1.4 TABULARFOUNDATIONMODELS
This category represents the most modern class of baselines: tabular foundation models trained on
synthetic tabular data. They generalize to new datasets in a zero-shot or in-context manner.
SurvPFN: (B ¨ohm et al., 2026)SurvPFN is a survival-specific Prior-Data Fitted Network (PFN)
based on the architecture of TabPFN v2, pretrained on synthetic censored survival tasks (sampling
from different distributions such as Weibull).
SurvivalPFN (Qi et al., 2026):SurvivalPFN performs amortized Bayesian survival inference by us-
ing the observed covariates, event times, and censoring indicators as in-context examples to directly
predict posterior event-time distributions for new individuals based on the CausalPFN architecture.
Survival-IC (Seletkov et al., 2026):Survival In-Context (SIC) is a prior-fitted foundation model
pretrained on synthetic survival datasets to approximate Bayesian posterior predictive inference and
based on the architecture of TabICL.
TFM4Survival (Kim et al., 2026):TFM4Survival adapts pretrained tabular foundation models such
as TabICL to censored time-to-event data through a sampling-based formulation, allowing existing
TFMs to perform survival prediction without survival-specific pretraining.

B.3 ISSUES FOR SOME METHODS
Some baselines failed to complete their hyperparameter search or final fit on a small subset of
datasets, for reasons intrinsic to the method rather than to the benchmark protocol (e.g., a train-
ing run that diverged numerically, or a step of the method’s own pipeline that is only defined for
datasets larger than the fold at hand). We assign the method a trivially achievable score on each
dataset on which it did not complete: an IBS of1(the maximum attainable value in expectation if
Gis estimated perfectly) or a concordance index of0.5(no better than chance), matching the metric
being ranked. All critical-difference diagrams and pairwise win-rate figures are therefore always
computed over the full 63-dataset held-out cohort. Methods with at least one imputed dataset are
marked with an asterisk in the critical-difference diagrams and with a hatched bar in the win-rate
figures; Table 5 lists the affected (method, dataset) pairs and their cause.

polynomial map, ported from SurvivalPFN’s prior
SIC priorsicextended-hazards law with a per-dataset hazard-family
draw, ported from SIC’s pretraining recipe
Recipe axes.Seven arms each flip exactly one flag off the control, holding
generation type=pofixed (Table 10). Two of the axes (discretization, administrative
censoring) are already off in the control, so we ablate by turning themonrather than removing them
– the same one-knob-at-a-time principle, applied in the direction the control recipe permits.

C.0.2 EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Arms are evaluated at their final checkpoint on frozen, held-out validation datasets. To account for
varying dataset scales and censoring rates, raw metrics are min–max normalized per dataset (100 =
best arm, 0 = worst arm) before averaging. The primary ranking metric is the mean Integrated Brier
Score (IBS).
