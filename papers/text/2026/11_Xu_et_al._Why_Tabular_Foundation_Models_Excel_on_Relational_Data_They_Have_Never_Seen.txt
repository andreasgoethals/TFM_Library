ABSTRACT
Given a relational database (RDB) storing heterogeneous tabular information, how
can we predict missing (or future) values in some target column of interest? As
candidate targets are numerous in enterprise settings, it is preferable to avoid
learning a new model from scratch each time there is a new prediction task. Frozen
foundation models (FMs) based on in-context learning (ICL) and RDB-specific
pre-training provide a viable solution, but the ideal design still remains largely
unresolved. Perhaps unexpectedly, preliminary evidence has recently shown that
existingsingle-table FMs(aka TFMs), when combined with suitable parameter-free
relational encoders, can be quite competitive in practice despitezero exposure to
RDB pre-training or relational data of any kind. In this work we first empirically
confirm this phenomenon with a more diverse array of experiments. We then turn
to our main contribution, namely, surfacing multiple complementary explanations
for why TFMs behave so effectively. These explanations ultimately hinge on
innate synergies whereby parameter-free encoders map relational effects to fixed-
length embeddings that loosely mimic distributions for which TFMs were originally
designed; in so doing, desirable single-table properties transfer to relational settings
(e.g., convergent posterior predictive distributions). Collectively, our analysis
forges new pathways for improving RDB FMs, particularly when we prioritize
interpretability and inevitable integration within agentic systems.
1 INTRODUCTION
Relational databases (RDBs), housing collections of interrelated tables, are indispensable across enter-
prise applications and e-commerce platforms (Garcia-Molina et al., 2009). From a machine learning
standpoint, the information stored within just a single RDB often contains countless possibilities for
predictive modeling, covering targets such as customer retention, user churn, or click-through rates
(Dave et al., 2014; Motl & Schulte, 2015; Ni et al., 2019; Zykov et al., 2022). Of course retraining a
new model from scratch to address each new predictive task of interest may be prohibitively laborious
or expensive, which motivates a new class of foundation models (FMs) specifically tailored for RDBs.
Among others, these vary from LLM-based approaches that digest serialized RDB content (Wydmuch
et al., 2024), to specialized Transformers pre-trained based on in-context learning (ICL) (Fey et al.,
2025; Hudovernik et al., 2026; Ranjan et al., 2026c; Wang et al., 2026), the common theme in each
case being afrozenarchitecture capable of making predictions involving arbitrary unseen RDBs.
As a robust and scalable alternative, it has recently been demonstrated thatsingle-table FMs(aka
TFMs), when combined with suitable parameter-free subgraph encoders for capturing multi-table
relational context, can perform well without requiring any RDB-specific pre-training (Grinsztajn
et al., 2026; Hayler et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026). After presenting background RDB context in Section
2, our work herein centers around confirming and explaining this unexpected performance via:
• Comprehensive demonstration.We provide thorough empirical comparison of wide-varying
RDB foundation models across diverse benchmarking tasks. These results from Section 3 solidify
the surprising effectiveness of TFMs repurposed for relational data.
• Explanatory theory.In Sections 4–7, we elucidate multiple complementary factors that help
explain this phenomenon and broader merits of TFMs. Specifically, we show that TFM-based
approaches: (i) naturally handle varying degrees of relational context, forming a continuum from

2 BASICS OFRDB FOUNDATIONMODELS
The goal of an RDB foundation model is to retain applicability across unseen RDBs, ideally with no
retraining for each new predictive task. Beyond direct LLM-prompting approaches (Wydmuch et al.,
2024), we now discuss recent pipelines designed to natively handle RDB/tabular data.

RDB predictive modeling generalizes this canonical ML scenario by integrating an additional set of

To make use of relational context for predictive tasks, the basic strategy is to first convert a given
labeled RDB D:={T,R,y} to a heterogeneous graph G. This typically involves treating each table

i (Cvitkovic, 2020;

exclude nodes with time-stamps later than xi. These subgraphs form the input to an RDB encoder

parametersϕ. This facilitates computation offixed-lengthembeddingsz i =g ϕ[GH(xi)]∈R dz.

Retrofitting TFMs with a relational encoder.Given the growing range of single-table FMs
(Hollmann et al., 2025; Jingang et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025b;a), it is natural to
consider combining with aparameter-freeencoder gpf, for flattening RDB neighborhoods into fixed-
length tabular features, such that the entire pipeline requires no RDB-specific training/pre-training
(Grinsztajn et al., 2026; Hayler et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026) (a conversion akin to U k ←T k, as
adopted by the pre-trained ICL-based model from Hudovernik et al. (2026) described above, may
be optionally considered as an additional pre-processing step, but we do not pursue this possibility
further). Herein we aim to confirm and explain the effectiveness of these simple approaches relative
to more complex alternatives.

Results.We compare across a suite of six diverse benchmarks from the appendix, each composed

Why are TFMs so competitive on RDBs?It is initially reasonable to assume that pre-training
RDB-specific FMs using actual RDB data (real or synthetic) is essential for achieving strong inference-
time performance on unseen relational data. So how can TFMs paired with some gpf possibly perform
well, as we have confirmed in Figure 1. The high-level argument from Xu et al. (2026) is that prior to
3

seeing ICL samples, a data-agnostic encoder is incapable of adjudicating column roles, which may
vary from dataset to dataset (or even task to task within a given RDB). This phenomenon advantages
RDB encoders that only compress verticallywithinhigh-dimensional columns (where shared units
facilitate interpretable aggregation), not horizontallyacrosscolumns, where relevance is largely
indeterminate without adequate label information. Conditioned on this restriction to vertical (intra-
column) compression, Xu et al. (2026) detail how gpf can result in minimal loss of expressiveness,
while naturally pairing with existing TFMs to form a complete RDB pipeline. While promising as an
analytical starting point, there remains considerable ambiguity regarding the rationale and surprising
empirical efficacy of this simple recipe. In the remainder of this work we take several disambiguating
steps, beginning with a discussion of the blurry line between single tables and RDBs, where TFMs
already work well on the former.
4 SINGLETABLES ANDRDBS ON ACONTINUUM
Inducing RDBs from single tables.Conventionally, TFMs and benchmarking (i.e., single-table
regimes) are treated separately from their RDB counterparts. But as we now examine, infinitesimal
adjustments to an initial single-table setup facilitate gradual transitioning to multi-table relational
scenarios, making any strict partitioning somewhat arbitrary. Concretely, beginning with a single-
table scenario, meaning K= 1 and features T 1 =X , we can trivially transform these data into two
or more tables with arbitrarily-small, labeled cross-row relational dependencies as follows:
1. Select any categorical column of T 1; w.l.o.g. assume B categories. If no categorical columns
exist, then we simply chose a numerical column and quantize intoBbins.
2. Form a new auxiliary table T 2 with a single length-B column, where elements are the unique
categories obtained from step 1 above. Treat the lone column withinT 2 as a PK, and the original
categorical column withinT 1 as an FK.
3. To induce more complex schema, we may optionally repeat steps 1–2 using otherT 1 columns.
4. Form subgraphs GH(x) seeded at any row x; provided H≥2 , these neighborhoods will include
multiple nodes associated with different rows of X. This is because the first hop reaches an
induced T k (k >1 ), while any second hop returns to T 1. In doing so, cross-row relational
dependencies are introduced via the edges withinG H(x)(Gan et al., 2024).

represents an informative hurdle for RDB FMs to pass. It is also worth noting that on three regression
tasks, RDB FMs actually surpass the performance of the non-relational baseline, and the RDBLearn
average regression performance modestly exceeds TabPFN-v3 (see the appendix).

6 ADEQUATEENCODEREXPRESSIVITY EVEN WITHNO PARAMETERS
We next address possible expressivity limitations associated with gpf usage, the key ingredient which
allows TFMs to avoid any RDB-specific training or pre-training. A natural presumption here is that
parameter-free encoders place a potentially-burdensome restriction on the types of RDB neighborhood
representations available for constructing ICL samples. We analyze this concern through a case study
involving interaction terms, where we find that the added expressivity of parameterized encodersgϕ
need not provide an unequivocal advantage over the much simplerg pf adopted by TFMs.
Interaction terms as a case study.Prior work has noted that parameter-free encoders that process
RDB columns individually (as mentioned in Section 3 and adopted by RDBLearn/TabPFN-Rel)
are incapable of handling interaction terms between columns in auxiliary tables. Along these lines,
consider the following representative example of this ostensible limitation discussed by Hudovernik
et al. (2026); Huang et al. (2026). Suppose we are given an RDB with only two tables: A labeled
target tableT 1 =yand a child tableT 2 = [a,b]composed of two binary feature columnsaandb.
A PK associated with each row of T 1 is linked by FKs to one or more rows of T 2, where Ri denotes
the set of rows within T 2 that have FKs pointing to the PK associated with row i in T 1. Ground-truth
labels are then constructed to satisfy
yi = max
j∈Ri
[aj ∧b j]∀i.(7)
Clearly the element-wise interactions aj ∧b j determine the label, and so the argument goes, excluding
such interactions is disproportionately problematic within any gpf that encodes columns separately as
advocated by Xu et al. (2026). We provide three counter-points to this concern:
Alllossy encoding involves negative counter-examples.The labels computed via (7) involve a
binary function over a 2|Ri|-dimensional input space, giving 22|Ri| = 4|Ri| possible input config-
urations.But this is just one possible function sharing this input range. Assuming no permutation
invariance across rows, there are2(4|Ri |) distinct binary functions expressible over the same input
space. Without external knowledge mandating which function is correct, an encoder that fails to
losslessly preserve the entire input range will make it impossible to guarantee that a subsequent
decoder can determine the correct labeling rule; a single missing bit can flip any labeling rule leading
to errors within an arbitrary downstream classifier.
More broadly, all lossy encoding,whether parameterized or not, opens up a set of inevitable counter-
examples, whereby learning the correct function is infeasible; see Xu et al. (2026)[Proposition 4.2] as
one representative scenario. This remains true even if task-specific labels are included within GH(x);
more on this in the appendix. Consequently, the primary differentiating consideration is not whether
counter-examples exist; rather, what matters is the relative frequency with which encoder-specific
counter-examples appear in real-world RDBs. 3 In general, functional expressivity is likely more
critical insupervisedscenarios, where trainable degrees-of-freedom can be mapped to task-specific
encoders that mitigate task-specific counter-examples (e.g., a particular set of interaction terms). But
for ICL-based FMs restricted to a fixed data-agnostic form, other factors such as interpretability or
sample complexity may matter more than parameterized expressivity per se.
3This is admittedly difficult to quantify, as we usually only have reported results on final predictive accuracy.

Arbitrary interactions challengeanyfixed encoder.Even if we restrict label generation to
functions of interaction terms similar to (7), parameterized encoders still do not have a clear-cut
advantage in real-world settings. This is because real-world auxiliary tables often have many
columns. Excluding all higher-order interactions, there will still exist a quadratically expanding
number of candidate interaction terms as the number of columns grow. Without knowing in advance
which are important, lossy encoder compression,parameterized or not, will create the potential for
prediction errors (i.e., there will always be cases where needed interaction signal depends on missing
information). The appendix analyzes how historical labels within GH(x) cannot generally resolve
this ambiguity under certain settings.
Parameter-free encoders can (sometimes) handle interactions.For arguments sake, the pre-
sumption thus far in this section has been that a column-wise parameter-free gpf cannot represent
interaction terms. But actually, this is not strictly true for the following two reasons. First, models
like RDBLearn or TabPFN-Rel can directly handle interactions between compressed representations
of individual columns; these representations form additional columns within Z∈R n×dz, and TFMs
can readily handle interactions between the constituent ICL features. Secondly, with enough aggrega-
tions of a suitable type, lossless compression is possible to enable interactions, at least in principle.
For continuous aggregators (Corso et al., 2020), the required cardinality is equal to the number of
real-valued elements being aggregated (not surprising), noting that these aggregators need not be
permutation invariant (e.g., they could be time-dependent, involve symmetry-breaking projections,
etc., for resolving ordered column values). But if arbitrary discontinuities are allowed (which is
admittedly not practically feasible to implement), even just a single aggregation is technically capable
of reconstructing the full set (Rubio-Madrigal & Burkholz, 2026); this conclusion follows from the
Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem (Schmidt-Hieber, 2021).
Interaction experiments.We conduct multiple tests to explore the impact of interaction terms;
see the appendix for full details. For the first, we generalize the setup of (7) to include 4 different
interaction types involving columns {a,b} in T 2; we then compare TFM-based RDBLearn against
the top non-TFM-based RDB FMs, KumoRFM-2 and RT-J. Figure 3 (left) shows the prediction
results as the width of T 2 is increased by adding uninformative columns. These distracting columns
follow the same distribution asa and b, and models do not know in advance which columns are useful,
raising estimation difficulty. Overall, we observe that RT-J does not work well in these settings, likely
because the implicit graph is disconnected, which can disrupt the collection of suitable ICL samples
(see Section 5.3). Meanwhile, RDBLearn is consistently competitive with KumoRFM-2, and actually
displays more stable performance across table width. This occurs because the aggregated features
from gpf can be combined by the subsequent TFM, at times forming usable approximations despite
not explicitly computing most interaction terms (see further details in the appendix). Although we do
not pursue further here, expanding the aggregation set within gpf can in principle reduce RDBLearn
prediction errors beyond what is shown.
For the second test, we begin with the rel-trial dataset (Robinson et al., 2024) and compute new
ground-truth labels via interactions between sets of auxiliary table columns. Results are shown in
Figure 3 (right) across 9 different column interaction pairs (x-axis) used for generating labels. While
these tasks are challenging for all methods, RDBLearn generally outperforms the others (agentic
results included on this bar plot are addressed in Section 7).

Feature discrimination in three stages.RDB predictive modeling depends heavily on the ability
to differentiate, on a task-specific basis, informative versus non-informative features, relational or
otherwise. There are (at least) three candidate stages where such feature screening may plausibly take
place: symbolically prior to any FM application, viareasoning agentswith access to task-dependent
meta-data or domain expertise (stage 1); locally at the RDB encoder level using whatever context is
available within GH(x) (stage 2); and globally at the decoder level in a purely data-driven manner,
using the full range of labeled ICL samples (stage 3). It is our assertion that TFM-based models
are particularly adept at utilizing all three stages ascomplements, particularly because their gpf can
reduce potential interference between stages. In support of this conjecture, we offer the following:
Stage 2 as a conduit from the symbolic to the data-driven.Given the previously stated capabilities
of stages 1 and 3, a plausible role for stage 2 becomes relatively transparent: Make sure any features

known recency biases. Of course numerous other pragmatic possibilities exist as well, which can be
iteratively explored by an agentic system, with robust and scalable validation facilitated by TFMs.
TFMs innately honor stage 1–3 roles.When we adopt TFMs, equipped with an appropriate gpf,
any designated golden feature from stage 1 can simply be appended to others in stage 2 without
deviating from the original tabular setting under which stage 3 pre-training was conducted. But
this need not be the case for existing RDB FMs with parameterized gϕ. For the latter, there is
no guarantee that any input dimension/feature, golden or not, will be preserved within the output
embedding. Moreover, if we simply append such features to the encoder output (bypassing the
parameterized encoder itself), we then introduce potential mismatch from the original RDB-specific
pre-training regime of these architectures. There is also the larger related issue ofinterpretability. An
agent can directly reason over the SQL-based aggregations used to form gpf and offer suggestions
regarding what useful factors might be missing. In contrast gϕ is much more opaque since the output
embeddings result from stacked Transformer layers, e.g., there is no obvious way for an agent to tell
if a valuable interaction term is attenuated or absent altogether.
Empirical corroboration.Consider the hypothetical scenario whereby an agent (stage 1) with
domain knowledge materializes a golden feature; namely, an interaction term which determines the
downstream label is added as a new column (see the appendix). Figure 3 (right) results show that
RDBLearn leverages this information to consistency boost performance in line with the three-stage
perspective from above. RT-J and KumoRFM-2 experience more modest gains, and in certain cases
do not capitalize at all on the provided golden feature (e.g., p-val ×onesided, p-val×disp-sd cases
from the figure), suggesting that the respective pipelines have somehow attenuated or underutilized
this golden feature. We further explore real-world implications in the appendix , whereby RDBLearn
with a single coding-agent prompt can match the performance of a SOTA multi-agent system (Huang
et al., 2026) that trains numerous modelssupervisedon a per-task basis. Moving forward, we also
emphasize that adefaultexpectation should be that any agentic system built on top of tabular base
models will likely exceed the benchmark performance of pipelines such as RDBLearn and TabPFN-
Rel-OSS. This is because as public open-source projects, their design choices represent an observable
starting point with known performance upon which to calibrate any additional enhancements, albeit
at the cost of significant leakage concerns discussed in the appendix.

8 TAKE-HOMEMESSAGE
Despite never seeing relational data (real or synthetic) during their pre-training stage, empirical
results indicate that TFMs paired with a simple gpf are competitive with the best alternative RDB
FM architectures. Although our core analyses provide multiple complementary rationales for this
phenomenon, we readily concede that advancements in the field may plausibly shift the balance
moving forward, hopefully catalyzed in part by our findings herein.
