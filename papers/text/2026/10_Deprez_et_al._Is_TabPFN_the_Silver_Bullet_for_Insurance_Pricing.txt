ABSTRACT
Modelling claim frequency and severity for non-life insurance pricing predominantly relies on gener-
alised linear models (GLMs), with gradient-boosted machines as the leading machine learning alter-
native. Tabular foundation models (TFMs) present a fundamentally different inference paradigm. By
pre-training on large collections of synthetic datasets, TFMs enable inference on new data through
in-context learning, without any dataset-specific preprocessing, fitting, or hyperparameter tuning.
This paper presents a first empirical evaluation of TabPFN for motor insurance pricing, benchmark-
ing two versions of TabPFN against GLM and XGBoost on two publicly available motor third-party
liability (MTPL) datasets. Our results show that TabPFN does not consistently outperform estab-
lished baselines: it falls behind the GLM on claim frequency, where its performance depends on the
size of the in-context training set, but it is competitive on claim severity, where data are scarcer. Its
inference times are substantially longer. Although tabular foundation models are promising, partic-
ularly in data-scarce settings, TabPFN is not yet ready to replace established actuarial methods for
claim frequency.

Frequency and severity are typically modelled with generalised linear models (GLMs) or generalised additive models
(GAMs) in practice [6, 17]. Both are highly transparent, given that their outputs are easily explained to stakeholders
based on the estimated model parameters [12].
Tree-based learners, such as decision trees and random forests, capture non-linearity and interactions automatically
while retaining transparency to some extent [8, 4], and can be used to construct a competitive GLM [14]. Deep learning
has been adopted more slowly. On tabular data including around 10k samples, tree-based methods outperform it [11],
and standard neural networks yield overly smooth predictions [17]. Research has integrated actuarial knowledge, as
in combined actuarial neural networks [24], the LocalGLMnet [23] or the credibility transformer [22, 20]. A shared
limitation of these methods is the need for dataset-specific preprocessing, fitting, and hyperparameter tuning.
A fundamentally different inference paradigm has recently emerged with tabular foundation models (TFMs) [21, 16].
Based on Prior-data Fitted Networks (PFNs), a class of transformer-based models pre-trained on multiple datasets,
these models allow for in-context learning [19]. A promising TFM is TabPFN [15, 16], which is pre-trained on
millions of synthetic tabular datasets and requires no dataset-specific fitting (Section 2).

Hollmann et al. [16] illustrate that TabPFN with default hyperparameters on average outperforms tuned state-of-the-art
tabular models. However, these benchmarks only evaluate prediction accuracy and thus provide no evidence on its
suitability for insurance pricing.
One risk of using TabPFN out of the box is that insurance pricing is reduced to a generic regression problem.
TabPFN does not yet incorporate all aspects of exposure, sparse claim counts, heterogeneous conditional variance,
and distribution-specific loss functions, which are crucial for frequency and severity pricing.
To our knowledge, in the peer-reviewed literature, only Chu et al. [5] have applied TabPFN in insurance, framing
cross-selling of health insurance as a binary classification task. Whether TabPFN is a competitive alternative for
the core actuarial problem of pricing remains an open question. This paper presents a first empirical assessment
of TabPFN on non-life insurance pricing data, benchmarking two versions of it against GLM and extreme gradient
boosting (XGBoost) [3] on two publicly available motor third-party liability (MTPL) datasets.
2 TabPFN
TabPFN approximates Bayesian inference [19, 16]. Given a priorp(φ)over data-generating mechanismsφand a
training contextD= (X train,y train), the prediction for new dataX test is the posterior predictive distribution (PPD)

This differs from Bayesian inference in actuarial science, such as credibility theory and Bayesian GLMs [7], where a
prior is placed on the parameters of a fixed parametric model. TabPFN instead places a prior on entire data-generating
mechanisms, and the resulting posterior predictive distribution is approximated by a neural network rather than derived
analytically or by sampling. Hence, TabPFN has no explicit likelihood and no posterior distribution over interpretable
parameters. TabPFN learns to approximate the PPD once, during pre-training on millions of synthetic datasets gen-
erated from random structural causal models, by predicting held-out rows of each dataset given the other rows as
context [16, 10]. At prediction time, the weights are fixed and the training set is passed through the network to-
gether with the test rows. For regression, TabPFN outputs a discretised predictive distribution without parametric
assumptions [16], of which we use the mean, averaged by default over an ensemble of forward passes with different
preprocessing and feature orders.
We consider two versions of TabPFN. TabPFN-v2.6 attends across the features of each row and across the training
rows of each feature [16], and supports up to 100,000 samples and 2,000 features, so larger training sets must be
subsampled. TabPFN-v3 instead compresses each sample into a single row embedding before in-context learning,
trading feature capacity for sample capacity [10]. It scales to 1,000,000 samples provided that the feature count
remains below 200.
Both versions assume that the data resemble the synthetic prior, which is not tailored to insurance data, and that
observations are exchangeable and equally weighted. Moreover, TabPFN supports neither offsets nor observation
weights. In addition, the context size is limited, and the inference time grows with the context size, since the training
set is passed through the network again for every prediction.
A further limitation concerns licensing: the TabPFN code is released under the Apache 2.0 licence, but the weights
of v2.6 and v3 fall under Prior Labs’ non-commercial licences, which at the time of writing permit research and
evaluation, as in this study, but not commercial or production use. Insurers that want to deploy TabPFN for pricing
therefore need a commercial licence from Prior Labs.
3 Experimental Set-up
We benchmark TabPFN-v2.6 and TabPFN-v3 against a Poisson/gamma GLM and XGBoost on the French freMTPL2
and Belgian beMTPL97 datasets from the CASdatasets package [9]. These are public MTPL benchmarks that have
been used in several actuarial studies [23, 17, 14, 13, 22, 18, 4, 2], and they differ in size (about 678,000 and 163,000
policies), which allows us to examine how performance and inference time depend on the amount of training data.
Comparing both versions shows whether the newer architecture and its extended context size improve predictive
performance and computational efficiency.
TabPFN is designed to operate on raw inputs, so preprocessing is applied to GLM and XGBoost only. We apply ordinal
encoding for ordered categoricals, one-hot encoding for nominal categoricals, and no transformation of numerical
features. XGBoost uses the fixed hyperparameters of Henckaerts et al. [14] (maximum depth 3, 500 trees, learning

The models differ in how they account for exposure. GLM and XGBoost incorporate the claim countN i withlne i as
an offset, so that the expected number of claims is proportional to the exposure [26, 14]. TabPFN, however, supports
neither an offset nor observation weights, so exposure cannot enter the model in the same way. We include the exposure
ei as an input feature and take the claim countN i as the target [25]. We do not model the claim rateN i/ei directly,
since dividing by small exposures produces extreme values. The claim rate is obtained by evaluating every policy at a
full year of exposure, which places the predictions on the same annualised scale as those of the GLM and XGBoost.
Note that this is not equivalent to an offset: whereas the GLM and XGBoost impose proportionality between exposure
and the expected claim count, TabPFN learns the effect of exposure from the data. Another option would be to limit
the data to observations with exposure equal to 1, but we did not explore this further since it is not in line with how the
models would be used in reality.
For severity, the GLM and XGBoost are weighted by the number of claimsNi, whereas TabPFN is trained unweighted,
as it does not support observation weights. To account for the right-skewed claim amounts, TabPFN is fitted on
ln(1 +yi)using its built-in target transformation [25]. TabPFN maps its full predictive distribution back to the original
scale, and we take its mean as the predicted severity.
All models are trained and evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation, with performance summarised through the root
mean squared error (RMSE), distributional deviance, and computational cost. The RMSE is a purely predictive loss.
For frequency, it is computed on the claim ratesy i =N i/ei and weighted by the exposurese i; for severity, it is
computed on the average severitiesy i without weights.

frequency and severity modelling [26, 14]. Claim frequency is typically assumed to follow a Poisson distribution [26,

Insurance pricing models operate under latency constraints, as a quote requested online must be returned within sec-
onds, making inference time a critical operational metric. We therefore analyse computation time in addition to the
above performance metrics. For GLM and XGBoost we combine training and inference time. TabPFN reports only
inference time given that no training happens for in-context learning.
To analyse the role of the context size, we vary it for the frequency data, where each training fold contains approxi-
mately 542,400 (freMTPL2) and 130,600 (beMTPL97) policies. We evaluate context sizes of 2,000, 5,000, 10,000,
50,000 and 100,000, the latter being the maximum context size of TabPFN-v2.6, and additionally use the full training
fold for TabPFN-v3. The severity data are much smaller, with 24,944 (freMTPL2) and 18,276 (beMTPL97) policies
with at least one claim, so each training fold (approximately 20,000 and 14,600 policies) lies well within the maximum
context of both versions and is used in full.
All experiments are implemented in Python and run on an Intel Xeon Platinum 8360Y CPU and NVIDIA A100
SXM4 GPU of the Flemish Supercomputer Center. The implementation is available on GitHub (https://github.
com/B-Deprez/tabpfn_insurance).

Also in terms of Poisson deviance GLM performs best on both datasets. XGBoost outperforms all TabPFN-v2.6 con-
figurations and outperforms TabPFN-v3 at every context size except the full training fold on the French dataset, while
TabPFN-v2.6 outperforms XGBoost at every context size on the Belgian dataset. The deviance of TabPFN is more
sensitive to the context size than its RMSE. For TabPFN-v3 on the French data, the deviance improves almost steadily
with the context size, from 0.363 at 2,000 policies to 0.343 on the full training fold. For TabPFN-v2.6, in contrast,
it follows a non-monotonic pattern: performance worsens from 2,000 to 10,000 policies and only improves slightly
at 100,000. Similar non-monotonic patterns were observed by Baesens et al. [1] for TabPFN in their benchmark on
credit risk modelling. This likely reflects distributional mismatch between small subsamples and the full training fold,
and warrants further research into the underlying cause.
The results on the severity data paint a different picture (Table 2 and Figure 2). The GLM model performs the worst of
all on the French dataset, while the XGBoost model performs best, followed by TabPFN-v2.6. On the Belgian dataset,
TabPFN-v3 performs best while TabPFN-v2.6 performs worst.
The standard deviations reported in Table 2 show strong variance in performance across folds, especially on the French
dataset. Figure 2 illustrates that this is mostly influenced by one outlier. To mitigate this, we also look at the relative
performance compared to the GLM model.
Relative to the GLM, the results on the French dataset partly favour TabPFN-v2.6. It seems that TabPFN-v2.6 has a
better average performance than XGBoost in terms of RMSE, although the performance is still more spread out, with
some folds on which TabPFN outperforms XGBoost and some on which it does not. The relative gamma deviance on
average still favours XGBoost, but we see that this is due to one outlier. The results for TabPFN-v2.6 are much more
stable, and on most folds it outperforms XGBoost.
The conclusions on the Belgian dataset do not change much when going to relative performance. TabPFN-v3 is still
the best model over most folds.

Practical concerns extend beyond predictive power. Inference time of TabPFN grows steeply with context size (Fig-
ure 3), which may limit its deployment in high-throughput, latency-sensitive environments. Even at small context
sizes, TabPFN’s inference cost substantially exceeds the combined training and inference time of GLM and XGBoost,
reaching several minutes at the model’s capacity limit. Although these times cover scoring an entire fold rather than a
single quote, the gap indicates that currently only GLM and XGBoost are suitable for real-time pricing applications at
scale. We observe that the inference time is much lower for TabPFN-v3 compared to TabPFN-v2.6.
5 Conclusion
We present a first empirical evaluation of TabPFN for non-life insurance pricing on two MTPL datasets. TabPFN does
not consistently outperform established baselines. Although TabPFN is competitive on the smaller severity datasets,
it falls behind on frequency where GLM still performs best. TabPFN supports neither an offset nor observation
weights, so exposure enters as a feature and its effect must be learned. In practice, TabPFN thus offers a trade-off. It
requires no dataset-specific preprocessing, fitting, or hyperparameter tuning, returns a full predictive distribution, and
is competitive when data are scarce, as for severity. These advantages come at the cost of long inference times on
GPU hardware, a non-commercial licence for its weights, the lack of support for offsets and observation weights, and
the absence of transparent coefficients. Therefore, TabPFN cannot yet replace established actuarial methods for claim
frequency.
Future work should investigate fine-tuning foundation models on insurance data using actuarial expert knowledge, ex-
tending the benchmark to additional datasets and TFMs, comparing with further methods proposed for these datasets,
examining the downstream impact on premium setting and profitability, and analysing whether TabPFN recovers the
same risk drivers as GLMs or yields complementary insights.
Another important direction lies in developing tabular foundation models that handle exposure, sparse claim counts,
heterogeneous conditional variance, and distribution-specific loss functions, which are crucial for insurance pricing.
Although our results do not favour TabPFN for claim frequency pricing, foundation models may prove more useful
elsewhere in the insurance value chain, such as fraud detection using classification or anomaly detection, loss reserving
with run-off triangles, or mortality projections in life insurance.
