ABSTRACT
In-context-learning Tabular Foundation Models (TFMs) are uniquely suited to
scenarios with limited resources or privacy constraints, as they solve new tasks
without additional training. However, their architecture consists of multiple
transformer blocks, resulting in high computational and memory footprints.
Meanwhile, traditional alternatives like Gradient-Boosted Decision Trees (GB-
DTs) remain highly competitive and run efficiently on CPUs, suggesting that
tabular tasks may not inherently require deep architectures. This contrast raises
a fundamental question:can TFMs be simplified by removing architectural
redundancies without sacrificing performance?To answer this, we introduce
a closed-form, label-free affine translator that replaces a contiguous window of
up to 96% of the blocks, leaving the rest unchanged. Evaluating eight frontier
TFMs on 171 classification datasets, we reveal that tabular transformers are
vastly overparameterized: replacing half of a model’s blocks costs at most0.002
median AUROC across all architectures, with the half-depth TabFM still outper-
forming every other full-depth TFM and tuned GBDTs. Furthermore, different
model families are overparameterized in different ways: TabPFN-3 relies on its
encoders, maintaining performance regardless of which single block is retained.
Other single-axis models spread computation thinly, needing only their first
block, while dual-axis models concentrate it in one critical block. Ultimately,
removing depth saves up to 1.5B parameters, speeds up GPU inference by up
to6×at a median cost of0.03AUROC, while a single-block TabPFN-3 serves
cached queries2.4×faster using6×less memory at negligible cost.

In-context-learning (ICL) TFMs offer a powerful approach for tabular prediction: they solve new
tasks with no additional training, handle heterogeneous data, and require no preprocessing or hyper-
parameter tuning. Recent models such as TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2025; Grinsztajn et al., 2026),
TabICL (Qu et al., 2025), and TabDPT (Ma et al., 2025) achieve strong performance across diverse
benchmarks. However, this zero-shot convenience comes at a high computational cost. Current
TFMs stack 12 to 24 identical transformer blocks on top of their encoders. This depth imposes
heavy memory footprints and high inference latency, limiting deployment on resource-constrained
hardware such as local clinical servers. Meanwhile, traditional alternatives like GBDTs remain
highly competitive on these same tasks while running efficiently on CPUs (Grinsztajn et al., 2022;
McElfresh et al., 2023). The success of these shallower, tree-based models suggests that typical tab-
ular tasks do not inherently require deep hierarchical composition. If that is true, much of the deep
architecture inside current TFMs may be computationally redundant. This contrast raises a funda-
mental question:can we retain the zero-shot convenience of TFMs while approaching the efficiency
of simpler models by removing architectural redundancy?
To answer this, we must first understand how these models utilize their depth. While observational
metrics such as pairwise representation similarity and attention profiling reveal signs of architec-
tural redundancy, the correspondence between these signals and actual replaceability is inconsistent
across models. We therefore locate redundancy by intervening on the architecture itself: we intro-
duce a closed-form, label-free affine translator that bypasses a contiguous window of transformer
blocks. If the output distribution remains stable, the skipped blocks were performing computations
that a simple linear map can absorb; if the output collapses, they were performing necessary non-
linear composition. We design this translator with a per-feature parameterization to support dual-
axis attention, and select the optimal bypass window by minimizing the forward KL divergence on
a small calibration split1.
Evaluating 8 TFMs from 6 model families across 171 classification datasets, we show that current
tabular architectures are overparameterized. Replacing half the blocks across all models results in a
minimal median drop of at most0.002AUROC, with up to 97% of datasets staying within one point
of the full model’s performance. Pushing compression to a single block maintains high performance
for TabPFN-3, Orion-Bix, and TabICL, which stay within a median0.007AUROC of their full-
depth counterparts. The savings translate directly to hardware efficiency: halving the depth doubles
the throughput of the transformer stack, and with a cached context, per-query GPU latency drops
by1.5×–1.9×at Half and2.4×–5.1×with one block kept. We show that naively dropping blocks
causes model collapse, and early exit performs worse than the label-free translator in most configu-
rations. The translator can also be reused across similar datasets. For example, a half-depth TabICL
translator fitted on MIMIC-III (Johnson et al., 2016) transfers to the eICU (Pollard et al., 2018)
dataset with an AUROC penalty of just0.004(against0.003for a local fit), though transferability
diminishes between datasets with different schemas (e.g., Heart and Diabetes, Section B.14).
Finally, through a controlled pre-training study on nanoTabPFN (Pfefferle et al., 2025), we inves-
tigate whether compression matches natively shallow training. We observe that a 12-layer model
compressed toklayers matches the performance of a model trained from scratch at depthk(within
0.011AUROC). Furthermore, a natively trained 6-layer model matches the AUROC of the full 12-
layer baseline, indicating that depth is over-provisioned in this specific setting. Whether this struc-
tural redundancy scales to significantly deeper architectures remains an open question for future
work. For existing models, our proposed approach offers a practical method to achieve the inference
efficiency of a shallow architecture without the computational cost of retraining from scratch.
2 RELATED WORK
Tabular Transformers.The development of tabular transformers began with task-specific archi-
tectures. Early adaptations like FT-Transformer (Gorishniy et al., 2021) and SAINT (Somepalli
et al., 2021) apply standard attention mechanisms to supervised learning, while TabNet (Arik &
Pfister, 2021) uses sequential sparse routing for step-wise feature selection. Since then, the field
has moved toward general-purpose foundation models, with a strong focus on in-context learning
TFMs. TabPFNv2 (Hollmann et al., 2025) and TabPFN-3 (Grinsztajn et al., 2026) scale prior-data
fitted networks to handle heterogeneous columns and mixed data types. TabICL (Qu et al., 2025;

2026) processes tabular structures via set-transformers. TabDPT (Ma et al., 2025) pre-trains on real
data, while TabFM (Kong et al., 2026) is pre-trained on synthetic data, and Mitra (Zhang et al.,
2025b) mixes diverse synthetic priors. Finally, the Orion family (Bouadi et al., 2025; 2026) and
LimiX (Zhang et al., 2025a) vary the attention pattern of the same in-context recipe (multi-scale
sparse, bi-axial and dual-axis cell attention).
Transformer Redundancy.Over-parameterization is a well-documented trait of vision and
language transformers, where dropping layers, attention heads, or tokens often yields minimal per-
formance degradation (Gromov et al., 2025; Men et al., 2025; Michel et al., 2019; Bolya et al., 2023;
Venkataramanan et al., 2024). In vision, Jacobs et al. (2026) replace deep transformers with a small
number of recurrent blocks, while Bolya et al. (2023) explicitly exploit token-level redundancy.
More closely related to our approach, Shopkhoev et al. (2025) and Cannistraci et al. (2026) show
that training-free linear maps can accurately bridge skipped block representations. Following the
recent emergence of ICL TFMs, several concurrent works have begun exploring depth and context
redundancy in the tabular domain. However, concurrent approaches fundamentally differ from
ours by requiring either additional pre-training or dataset-specific gradient updates. For instance,
Balef et al. (2026) pre-train a single-layer model from scratch to loop at inference, while Zab ¨ergja
et al. (2026) pre-train a predictor-compressor pair to condense context rows into latent vectors.
Among post-training approaches, TACTICL (Koshil et al., 2026) replaces transformer layers with
lightweight MLP adapters trained via gradient descent on the downstream task. Similarly, (Tanna
et al., 2026) relies on labeled data to train task-specific student models, while early-exit methods
(K¨uken et al., 2025) pre-train new layer-wise decoders on prior data. In contrast, our approach uses
a single, closed-form affine map, requiring no non-linear adapters, gradient updates, or labeled data

Per-Architecture Parameterization.Single-axis self-attention architectures such as TabFM and
TabPFN-3 operate on sequence tensors where items are processed independently, requiring a single
shared transformation matrix(W,b). Conversely, dual-axis models such as TabPFNv2 and LimiX
attend across both rows and features, producing hidden tensors of shape(·, n features, d). Applying
a single shared matrix across this tensor would mix feature representations and disrupt axis align-
ment. Thus, for dual-axis architectures, we fit a distinct affine map(W f ,b f )for each feature index
f∈ {1, . . . , nfeatures}(additional details in Section A.3). This expands the total translator param-
eterization ton features(d2+d), which scales dynamically with the dataset’s feature dimensionality.
However, in some cases (e.g., TabPFNv2), these parameters can be largely absorbed.
Absorbing the Map.Because the map is affine, it can be absorbed into an adjacent affine layer.
However, since transformer blocks contain non-linear operations, this operation is restricted to the
network boundaries. If the window ends at the final block and the classification head applies a linear

4 LOCATINGCOMPUTATIONALREDUNDANCY
Before evaluating the performance of compressed models, we investigate how computation is dis-
tributed across the transformer blocks. We first examine representation and attention dynamics, then
we perform a per-block intervention to accurately locate redundant blocks to replace.
Experimental Setup.We evaluate 8 pre-trained TFMs from 6 model families (TabPFN, TabICL,
TabDPT, TabFM, Orion and LimiX) with frozen weights across 171 classification datasets: 163
from four deduplicated benchmark suites (OpenML-CC18 (Bischl et al., 2021), TabZilla (McElfresh
et al., 2023), TabArena (Erickson et al., 2025), Grinsztajn (Grinsztajn et al., 2022)) and eight clinical
datasets including MIMIC-III (Johnson et al., 2016) and eICU-CRD (Pollard et al., 2018). Each
dataset is split into train, calibration, and test, and run at 3 seeds. For deterministic extraction,
where possible, prediction-time ensembling is reduced to a single member, and LimiX-16M’s input
preprocessing is fitted once on the support rows and then frozen (Section A.3). Additional details,
including datasets and models details, are in Section A, while additional results are in Section B.
Representation Similarity.Figure 2 shows the full pairwise cosine similarity between every pair
of block representations on a single forward pass using MIMIC-III. The resulting matrices vary
drastically across architectures. For example, from block 3 on, consecutive TabFM representations
have cosine similarity≥0.99, consistent with the concurrent findings of Balef et al. (2026) that
other TFMs representations stabilize early. In contrast, TabDPT shows broad similarity in its middle
blocks and Orion-MSP in its last ones, while TabPFN-3 exhibits a smooth transition with no sharp
boundaries. Finally, TabICL’s final block is the most dissimilar from every other block, while results
in Figure 11 show that it is fully replaceable by the affine translator. Representation similarity
therefore does not accurately predict replaceability.

Attention Patterns.If the depth of a tabular TFM is redundant, one might expect later blocks to
simply stop specializing and repeatedly retrieve the same context rows. However, attention statistics
on two diverse datasets, MIMIC-III and Higgs, show a different behaviour: the five most-attended
keys per query overlap by a Jaccard index of only0.10–0.19between consecutive blocks (vs.0.005
randomly). Additionally, TabPFN-3’s first block shares almost no keys with later ones (Section B.1).
Nor does attention specialize with depth as in language models. TFMs attend almost uniformly
over their context at every block (averaging 81–95% of maximum entropy, and≥70% at every
block except TabFM’s read-out block), whereas Qwen3-4B’s attention is sharply peaked and highly
variable (Figure 3 and Section B.1). Ultimately, although each block retrieves a distinct set of
rows, the entropy profile remains persistently flat. Consequently, we must look beyond attention
mechanisms or embedding similarities to understand when the network becomes redundant.

Per-Block Sensitivity.To locate the redundant blocks directly, we use the affine translator itself
as diagnostic tool. Specifically, we intervene by replacing individual blocks with the translator and
measuring the resulting change in AUROC (Figure 4). Notably, the surviving blocks behave just as
they do in the full model (attention entropy shifts by≤2%; Section B.1). For single-axis models,
no single block is critical and every replacement stays within0.007AUROC of the full model. In
contrast, dual-axis models each have exactly one critical block (i.e., block 0 for TabPFNv2 and
block 3 for LimiX-16M), while every other block stays within0.043and0.009, respectively. In
short, evaluated one block at a time, almost the entire stack is affine-replaceable and the non-affine
computation that remains sits in a single block of the dual-axis models. More results in Figure 11.

Ablations.One could argue that the skipped blocks are simply inactive, making our translator
unnecessary. To test this, we evaluate dropping the same blocks without applying the affine map,
effectively treating the skipped section as an identity function (Figure 5,right). Under this baseline,
performance collapses for most models down to chance level (Table 9). The skipped blocks are
therefore not idle, but the closed-form map recovers their computation.
Because an affine approximation suffices, we also evaluate a standard early-exit baseline: fitting
a supervised logistic regression classifier directly to the representations right before the skipped
window, thereby discarding the remaining blocks and the original head. Despite having access to
the support labels, this shortcut underperforms in most configurations. It loses≈0.02AUROC at
every depth, whereas our label-free translator matches or outperforms it in 81% of the 2,664 tested
configurations (Table 16). Finally, repeatedly looping the retained block also fails to substitute the
affine map (Table 17). Mapping intermediate representations into the space the remaining blocks
and the frozen head expect therefore beats training a new head from scratch, even though the new
head sees labels.

e.g.One-blockTabFM drops about 6 GB of fp32 weights. Context caching removes this bottleneck,
yielding query speed-ups of up to5.1×. On CPUs, where the blocks dominate the run time, end-
to-end speed-ups reach12.4×(Table 23). The same gains apply to regression, which uses the same
backbones: atHalf, where regression is near-lossless, the lightweight models accelerate by1.5×–
1.8×on GPU, and atOne-blockTabFM sheds 1.5B parameters for a median∆R 2 of only−0.022.
7 COMPRESSION VS. PRE-TRAININGSHALLOWARCHITECTURES
If up to 96% of a tabular transformer’s layers can be safely approximated, a natural follow up
question would be: why not simply pre-train a shallow model from scratch? To analyze this, we
test whether compressing a model matches the performance of a natively shallow one using the
nanoTabPFN reference implementation (Pfefferle et al., 2025)2. We train the model across varying
depthsL∈ {1,2,3,6,12}, each with an embedding dimension of 96 and 4 attention heads, for 5,000
steps across three random seeds (see Section B.17 for configurations). We evaluate these models
on four binary clinical datasets (Diabetes, Heart, Breast Cancer, SUPPORT2), strictly operating in
the prior-fitting regime (100 support rows, top 5 F-score features, 1,000 test rows). We compare
these natively shallow models against the 12-layer model compressed tokblocks using our per-
feature translator. Although scaled down (1.4M parameters), this proxy model provides a controlled
environment to study the architectural origins of redundancy.

8 CONCLUSION
We introduced a closed-form, label-free affine translator to show that TFMs are overparameterized.
Across eight models from six families and 171 classification datasets, replacing half the transformer
blocks with a single affine map costs at most0.002median AUROC, and isolating a single block
remains competitive on most datasets. Per-block analysis shows why this is possible: in single-
axis models no individual block is critical, and in dual-axis models the non-affine computation
sits in a single block, while attention remains diffuse across depth. Unlike early-exit methods that
discard the network’s tail and require labeled data to train a new classifier, our translator succeeds
by preserving the pre-trained head and accurately projecting intermediate states into its expected
input space. Furthermore, at half-depth, a fitted map can be reused as-is across similar datasets (e.g.,
MIMIC-III and eICU), and for TabICL and TabPFN-3 it keeps most of its benefit even on unrelated
datasets. These reductions remove up to 96% of the parameters with one block kept (1.5B for
TabFM), speed up end-to-end GPU inference by up to6×at a median cost of0.03AUROC, and let
a single-block TabPFN-3 serve cached queries2.4×faster with6×less memory at negligible cost.
Limitations and future work.Beyond demonstrating architectural redundancy, our analysis iden-
tifies specific conditions where extreme compression to a single block fails, such as maintaining
continuous target rankings in regression, and for dual-axis models whose map cannot be absorbed
(i.e., LimiX-16M), the per-feature map outweighs the removed blocks on very wide datasets. Ad-
ditionally, our comparison between compression and shallow pre-training is currently limited to a
1.4M-parameter nanoTabPFN. Whether this performance parity holds at production scale, where
pre-training shallow models from scratch is prohibitively expensive, remains an open question.
These limitations motivate the design of adaptive architectures that allocate network depth only
when strictly required. Given how replaceable most blocks are, a recurrent transformer that reuses
one block’s weights could supply that depth without the parameter count of current models. Finally,
the window could be selected under a hardware budget instead of a fixed width, minimizing the
calibration KL subject to a target memory or latency that also accounts for the cost of the map.

A.3 TRANSLATOR IMPLEMENTATION AND ABLATIONS
The following section provides additional details regarding Section 3.
Dual-axis models.For the dual-axis models (i.e., TabPFNv2, LimiX-16M), block outputs have
shape(n items, nfeatures, d)and the map for feature indexfis applied asH ′[·, f,·] =H[·, f,·]Wf +bf .
Withn features = 1, the per-feature variant reduces exactly to the single-(W, b)case, so this is a
generalisation of the shared linear translator rather than a different method. The number of feature
tokens is set by each model’s preprocessing (for LimiX, the frozen preprocessing below), sonfeatures
is the token count, not the column count.
LimiX.LimiX refits its preprocessing on everypredict()call on the concatenated rows, so
the feature count and column identity drift between the calibration and evaluation passes, and a
per-feature map would be applied to mismatched columns. We therefore fit the preprocessing once
on the support rows and freeze it: categorical detection, the categorical encoder and the constant-
column filter are fitted on the support set and only applied to the query rows, so the column layout is
identical in every pass. With the frozen preprocessing the full model’s predictions are identical on 7
of the 8 clinical datasets (−0.006on Heart) and the per-feature map applies unchanged. On the 153
14

suite datasets both run, it loses0.005on average at Half (median0.002, 83% of datasets within one
point) and0.052at One-block (median0.029), against0.003and0.098for TabPFNv2. A shared
(W, b), which does not need the freezing, is near-lossless at width 3–4 but collapses with one block
kept (−0.151on the clinical datasets): the collapse reflects an incompatibility between the map and
the preprocessing, not evidence that LimiX needs its depth.
Conditioning.Each ridge solve hasd+1unknowns per output column. Its rows are sequence
positions from one forward pass over the support and calibration sets, capped at 500 (all 216 on
Heart, the smallest dataset). The cap applies to positions only. TabPFNv2 and LimiX-16M fit one
solve per feature token, each over the same≤500positions. TabICL fits one(W, b)across all
8 ensemble members (up to 4,000 correlated rows). The solve is therefore over-determined for
TabPFNv2, LimiX-16M (d=192) and TabICL (d=512), slightly under-determined for TabPFN-3
and Orion (d=512), and under-determined for TabDPT (d=768) and TabFM (d=2,048). In the
under-determined cases the ridge penalty makes the fit well-posed, and the training residualεbe-
comes optimistic because the fit interpolates. All claims therefore rest on test AUROC, not onε.
The translator never sees test data or labels. Extraction and solve take 33–109 ms per window on
one GPU.
Calibration budget.The translator is fitted on at most 500 positions of one forward pass. To test
whether this is enough, we refit at the KL-selected windows onN∈ {15,30,50,100,250,500}ran-
dom subsets (three draws each,λchosen label-free as always) on five clinical datasets and compare
with the capped fit. For mild compression the budget is small: at width 3 every model is within0.005
of the capped fit byN=100, TabFM (d=2,048) and Orion-Bix fromN=15, TabICL, TabPFNv2,
Orion-MSP and LimiX-16M fromN=50, TabPFN-3 and TabDPT fromN=100, because the KL-
selected mild windows sit late in the stack, where representations change little. With one block kept

still helps Orion-MSP (+0.022) and TabFM (+0.010), and at most0.006for the others. About 100
unlabelled positions therefore suffice for mild compression and 250–500 for extreme compression,
far below the context the model already consumes (Figure 8).

B.1 ATTENTION ANALYSIS
Measuring entropy.We measure attention diversity via per-head entropy:H(a) =
− P
j aj loga j, averaged across heads, queries, and samples. An entropy nearlog(S)(whereS
is the number of keys) signals uniform attention, while low entropy signals specialization. Because
foundation models compute attention inside fused kernels that do not return weights, we recompute
the softmax from the model’s own projected queries and keys. We intercept the scaled-dot-product
kernel during one forward pass and attribute each call to the block executing it. This interception
includes the kernel’s mask and scale, and applies TabFM’s query-key normalization where relevant.
We use the 500 unlabelled calibration rows as queries and all support rows as keys. We exclude the
16

dual-axis models (TabPFNv2, LimiX) from this specific analysis because they interleave item and
feature attention.
Top-koverlap between blocks.During the same forward pass, we record the five keys with the
largest attention weight for every head across the 500 calibration queries. We then compare blocks
using the Jaccard index of these key sets. We evaluate this on MIMIC-III and Higgs (seed 0),
yielding 3,000 candidate rows (1,500 for TabDPT).
Matching heads by index across blocks yields a Jaccard index of just0.02–0.04for all evaluated
models. Pooling the heads of a block (roughly 33 distinct keys per query) yields overall overlaps of
0.11–0.12(TabICL),0.09(TabDPT),0.11–0.12(TabFM), and0.06–0.08(TabPFN-3). Consecutive
blocks share slightly more (0.10–0.19), but this remains vastly different from the0.005expected
from random sets. Notably, block 0 in TabPFN-3 shares almost no keys (0.01) with later blocks.
Ultimately, the attended neighborhoods change at every block.

B.16 EXTENDED HARDWARE MEASUREMENTS
Absorbing the translator into the encoder or the head.Section 3 explains how the map can be
absorbed by an adjacent linear layer. Encoder side (window starting at block0): in TabPFNv2 each
feature token ofH 0 is the output of a linear layer, soH 0Wf +b f is again a linear function of that
layer’s input, and the per-feature map is absorbed into a per-feature copy of the encoder’s last linear
layer. Head side (window ending at blockL): TabDPT’s head readsHL through a linear layer, which
absorbs the map with its shape unchanged. Every other model has a normalization at the relevant
end, which prevents absorption: TabPFN-3, TabICL, TabFM and both Orions normalize the stack
output before the head, and the encoders of TabPFN-3, LimiX and Orion-Bix end in a LayerNorm
or RMSNorm. Across the full sweep, the selected One-block window touches the absorbing end in
86% of dataset–seed pairs for TabPFNv2 and 97% for TabDPT. Table 22 verifies that absorption is
exact on the eight clinical datasets at three seeds (fp32 on the GPU): predictions with and without
absorption differ by at most4×10 −6, and every metric (AUROC, AUPRC, accuracy, balanced
accuracy, F1, Brier, ECE) agrees to within10 −5.
CPU deployment.Table 23 repeats Table 1 on a desktop CPU (Intel i9-14900K, fp32, batch of
512 queries, re-encode regime). There the transformer stack takes most of the forward pass for
the lightweight-encoder models, so their end-to-end speed-up approaches the stack’s (9.5–12.4×at
One-block), while the heavy-encoder models stay at1.2–1.8×.
