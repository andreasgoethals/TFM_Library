abstract
We compare six modeling methods for Loss Given Default (LGD). We ﬁnd that non-parametric methods
(regression tree and neural network) perform better than parametric methods both in and out of sample
when over-ﬁtting is properly controlled. Among the parametric methods, fractional response regression
has a slight edge over OLS regression. Performance of the transformation methods (inverse Gaussian and
beta transformation) is very sensitive to
e, a small adjustment made to LGDs of 0 or 1 prior to transfor-
mation. Model ﬁt is poor when e is too small or too large, although the ﬁtted LGDs have strong bi-modal
distribution with very small e. Therefore, models that produce strong bi-model pattern do not necessarily
have good model ﬁt and accurate LGD predictions. Even with an optimal e, the performance of the trans-
formation methods can only match that of the OLS.

1. Introduction
The expected credit loss rate on any pool of debt over any given
horizon can be expressed as the product of probability of default
(PD) and Loss Given Default (LGD). Therefore, LGD is one of the
two determining factors of the premium of risky bonds, credit de-
fault swap spreads, and credit losses. It is also one of the key
parameters in the Basel II framework that are used to calculate
banks’ regulatory capital requirements. 1 In spite of its obvious
importance, the number of studies on LGD has been relatively low,
although growing in recent years. Most of the LGD studies focus
on investigating the importance of various factors that affect LGD,
for example, contract characteristics, borrower characteristics,
industry conditions, and macroeconomic conditions. 2 Very few stud-
ies of LGD explore the alternative modeling methodologies.
LGD modeling methods are of interest because the distribution
of LGD can be very different from the normal distribution, which is
behind most of the commonly used statistical models. First, LGDs
for corporate exposures tend to have a bi-modal distribution (for
example, Schuermann (2004)). That is, the LGD is either very high
or very low. Second, LGD is bounded between 0 and 1, while
theoretically the predicted values from the ordinary least squares
(OLS) regression can range from negative inﬁnity to positive inﬁn-
ity. Despite this unconventional distribution, most studies on LGD
continue to use the OLS regression.

importance of LGD in credit risk analysis, a good understanding of

This study aims to ﬁll the gap in the understanding of the var-
ious methods to model LGD. We employ a large dataset comprising
of 3751 defaulted securities in the US from 1985 to 2008 to com-
pare various LGD modeling methods. Our sample includes both
bank loans and bonds. We examine a total of six methods, four
of which are parametric – ordinary least squares regression
(OLS), fractional response regression (FRR), inverse Gaussian
regression (IGR), and inverse Gaussian regression with beta
transformation (IGR-BT), and the other two are non-parametric –
regression tree (RT) and neural network (NN).
We ﬁnd that the non-parametric methods provide better ﬁt and
more accurate prediction than the parametric methods. Among the
parametric methods, although fractional response regression has a
slight edge over OLS, both methods perform reasonably well.
Performance of the two transformation regression methods (i.e.,
inverse Gaussian and inverse Gaussian with beta transformation)
is very sensitive to the choice of
e, a small value added to LGD of
0 and subtracted from LGD of 1 prior to transformation. Very small
or very large e’s can result in inferior model ﬁt. Our ﬁndings thus
suggest that these transformation methods need to be used with
caution. Although ﬁtted LGDs from all methods show some degree
of bi-modal distributions, the two transformation methods with
extremely small e’s generate bi-modal patterns with heavy concen-
trations near LGD of 0 and 1, even though these models yield poor
model ﬁt. This evidence suggests that a model’s ability to produce a
strong bi-modal distribution does not necessarily lead to accurate
LGD estimation.
All models, including the non-parametric methods when the
over-ﬁtting problem is under control, are able to generate quite
stable out-of-sample results. However, inferences on some instru-
ment-level explanatory variables are slightly different between
models, suggesting that these instrument-level variables may not
have a clear-cut linear relation with LGD. This ﬁnding implies that
the current industry practice, which relies solely on instrument-le-
vel variables to predict LGDs, may not be adequate. Additional vari-
ables and non-linear or non-parametric relations should also be
considered.

2.2. The transformation regressions
Because LGDs are bounded in the unit interval [0, 1], whereas
the predicted LGDs from an OLS regression are not bounded, cer-
tain transformation can be applied to LGDs before running the
regression and the ﬁtted LGDs from the regression are then trans-
formed back to (0, 1). We consider two commonly used transfor-
mation regressions – inverse Gaussian that addresses the [0, 1]
boundary, and beta transformation that considers the bi-modal
LGD distribution, in addition to the [0, 1] boundary.
2.2.1. Inverse Gaussian regression (IGR)
First, we transform LGDs from the unit interval (0, 1) to
(/C01 , 1) using an inverse Gaussian distribution function. We then
run an OLS regression using the transformed LGDs, and ﬁnally, we
transform the ﬁtted values back from ( /C01 , 1) to (0, 1) using the
Gaussian distribution function. We choose the Gaussian distribu-
tion function out of convenience.
2.2.2. Inverse Gaussian regression with beta transformation (IGR-BT)
In this approach, we assume that LGDs follow a beta distribu-
tion. First, we use the realized LGDs to estimate the beta distribu-
tion parameters a and b. The cumulative probabilities are then
calculated using these estimated beta distribution parameters
and transformed from (0, 1) to ( /C01 , 1) using an inverse Gaussian
distribution. We then run an OLS regression and transform the ﬁt-
ted values from OLS back from ( /C01 , 1) to (0, 1) using the Gaussian
distribution. We ﬁnally convert the probabilities back from uni-
modal Gaussian distribution to bi-modal beta distribution using
the inverse beta distribution. So this method is quite similar to
the inverse Gaussian regression, except that it assumes a beta dis-
tribution for LGDs and uses the beta distribution to pre-process
and post-process LGDs.
2.2.3. Treatment for LGD values of 0 and 1
Neither inverse Gaussian nor beta transformation is deﬁned
when LGD equals 0 or 1, and thus, we need to adjust LGD values
of 0 or 1 by adding or subtracting a small positive value e. To min-
imize LGD data distortion, a natural choice for e would be a very
small number. However, if e is very small, the transformed value
of LGD = 0 (or e) is a ﬁnite number very close to /C01 , while the
transformed value of LGD = 1 (or 1 /C0 e) is a ﬁnite number very
close to 1. If a substantial proportion of the observations have
LGDs equal to 0 or 1, the transformed values will have very large
spikes near /C01 or 1, which are likely to result in poor model ﬁt
and inaccurate predictions. On the other hand, larger e could lead
to larger differences between the actual LGDs and the LGDs used
for model estimation when LGDs equal 0 or 1, which is likely to re-
sult in poor model ﬁt and inaccurate LGD predictions, too. There-
fore, it is important to investigate model sensitivity to different
e’s and whether there exists an optimal e that balances the impact
of data distortion and extreme values on model performance.

2.3. Regression tree (RT)
Regression tree is popularized by Breiman et al. (1984) and can
be used to predict continuous dependent variables or categorical
predictor variables. We derive predictions from a few simple if-
then conditions and partition the dataset recursively into smaller
mutually exclusive subsets.
The split algorithm of a regression tree minimizes intra-subset
variance. It starts with a root node that contains all observations.
It then searches over all possible binary splits of all variables to
generate the smallest intra-subset variation of the LGD in the next
level of nodes. The predicted LGDs are the average value of the
LGDs in each leaf node. This process is repeated recursively until
no further reduction in variance is possible or certain stopping
criterion is met. Because LGDs are in the range of [0, 1] and predic-
tions are given by averages of LGDs in each leaf node, predicted
LGDs from a regression tree will be bounded in [0, 1].
Results from a regression tree are easy to interpret and commu-
nicate, which makes it very appealing to users. In addition,
regression trees can easily handle non-linearity because they
approximate non-linear functions by piece-wise constant ones.
Further, this method is fairly robust to outliers.
Although over-ﬁtting can be a problem for all methods, it is
more of a concern for non-parametric methods, including regres-
sion tree. Given the noise in any real data, determining when to
stop splitting the tree is important when building a regression tree.
For example, 9 splits applied to a dataset of only 10 observations
would result in perfect ﬁt in sample, but the resulting model is un-
likely to generate robust prediction out of sample. In general, near
perfect ﬁt can be achieved with a sufﬁcient number of splits, but an
overly complex tree cannot produce accurate out-of-sample pre-
diction. One way to alleviate the concern of over-ﬁtting is to ﬂoor
the number of observations in each leaf node. Further, it is impor-
tant to conduct cross-validation tests by applying the tree built
from one set of observations (development sample) to another
completely independent set of observations (validation sample).
If most of the splits in the development sample are driven by noise,
then the prediction on the validation sample would be poor.
We use 10-fold cross-validation to examine the usefulness of a
regression tree. We divide the entire sample randomly into 10
mutually exclusive subsets of roughly the same size and reserve
each of the 10 subsets as the validation sample with the model
estimated using the remaining 9 subsets. We report the sum of
SSE from each of the 10 validation subsets and R-squared based
on this sum of SSE. To be consistent, we also apply the same 10-
fold cross-validation to other modeling methods as well.

3. Sample
Our sample is from the Moody’s Ultimate Recovery Database
(URD). The data coverage is US corporate default events with over
$50 million in debt at the time of default. It has three alternative
approaches of calculating recovery: the settlement method, the
trading price method, and the liquidity event method. 5 The data-
base also provides Moody’s preferred method, which varies for each
default and is the one Moody’s consider the most representative of
the actual recovery. The most common preferred method is the
settlement method. We use the LGD numbers from the preferred
method for all our analysis. To obtain discounted ultimate recover-
ies, each nominal recovery in URD is discounted back to the last time
when interest was paid using the instrument’s pre-petition coupon
rate. In total, we have 3751 observations from 1985 to 2008.
Panel A in Table 1 shows the number of LGD observations,
mean, median, and standard deviation by year. There are more
observations in years 2001 and 2002, coinciding with the high de-
fault rates in these 2 years. The mean LGD ranges from a low of
19.16% in 2007 to a high of 85.61% in 1985. The median LGD
reaches a low of 0 in 2006–2007. As is shown in the last row of
the panel, the standard deviation of the mean LGD by year is
15.19%, while that of the median LGD by year is 25.29%; these
numbers are low relative to the overall mean and median at
45.29% and 44.23%, respectively. However, the apparently low var-
iance of LGDs from year to year does not necessarily suggest that
the LGD is highly predictable for individual bonds or loans. The
standard deviation of LGDs in each year is in the range of 30–
40%, suggesting that there is a wide range of variations in LGDs
across individual instruments. The same conclusion can be found
in Fig. 1 , which shows that LGDs in our sample are quite widely
spread out, with a heavy concentration at both ends in the inter-
vals of [0, 0.1) and [0.9, 1]. This bi-modal distribution implies that
upon default, the most likely outcome is losing almost nothing
(over 30% chance) or losing almost everything (over 15% chance).

4. Results
This section compares results from different modeling methods
for LGD. We use the same set of independent variables across all
methods except for the regression tree, for which we include three
additional variables (percentage above, industry default rate, and
aggregate distance-to-default). This is because all methods except
for the regression tree could suffer from the multi-collinearity
problem as these three variables are highly correlated with the
other explanatory variables.

4.2. Fractional response regression (FRR)
Results from FRR are qualitatively similar to those from the
OLS.10 The exceptions are (1) senior secured bonds and the senior
subordinated bonds seem to have signiﬁcantly higher LGDs than
the base instrument (subordinated bonds), 11 and (2) the collateral
types of guarantees and intellectuals are not signiﬁcantly related
to LGD. Similar to OLS, the 10-fold cross-validation R-squared and
SSE from FRR are quite close to those in sample with low standard
deviations, suggesting that the underlying relationship is rather sta-
ble. Further, both the in-sample and out-of-sample R-squared are
higher and the SSEs are lower for FRR than for OLS. These results
indicate that FRR yields better model ﬁt than OLS.
We also plot the histogram of FRR-ﬁtted LGDs in Fig. 2. It is clear
that FRR-ﬁtted LGDs do a better job than OLS-ﬁtted LGDs in
mimicking the actual LGD distribution. We ﬁnd high concentra-
tions in the intervals [0.2, 0.3) and [0.7, 0.8). Not surprisingly, all
ﬁtted values from FRR fall within [0, 1].
4.3. Transformation regressions (IGR and IGR-BT)
We ﬁrst investigate in Table 4 results using different e’s. This ta-
ble shows that results from the transformation regressions are very
sensitive to the choice of e. For both IGR and IGR-BT, the model ﬁt
improves initially as e increases from 1.0E /C0 11 to 0.05 and then
deteriorates afterwards; the latter part is not surprising as a higher
e creates larger differences between the actual LGDs and the LGDs
used in model estimation. With e =1 E/C0 11, IGR yields an R-squared
of 0.171 and IGR-BT leads to an R-squared of 0.111. The numbers
are drastically lower than those of OLS and FRR reported in Table
3. SSEs are also much higher among the transformation methods.
The highest R-squared and lowest SSE for both methods (both
in-sample and 10-fold cross-validation) is achieved at e = 0.05,
suggesting that it may be the optimal epsilon for this sample. At
e = 0.05, the model ﬁt from IGR and IGR-BT matches that of OLS
and is slightly worse than that of FRR. Note that the standard
deviations of R-squared and SSE from the 10-fold cross-validation
are very high at very low e’s for both transformation methods. This
result suggests that extreme values generated by very small e’s
make the transformation methods unstable.
Table 5 reports the detailed results from the transformation
methods under e = 0.05. Results from the two transformation meth-
ods are quite similar. The main differences in coefﬁcient estimates of
the explanatory variables between these methods and those from
either OLS or FRR relate to the instrument type senior subordinated
bonds and collateral types guarantees, intellectual, inter-company
debt, and other. These variables are non-signiﬁcant under either
OLS or FRR and become signiﬁcant in Table 5 under both IGR and
IGR-BT. The ﬁnding that inferences on some instrument-level vari-
ables are slightly different between models implies that the instru-
ment-level variables may not have a clear-cut linear relation with
LGD. Therefore, the current industry practice, which relies solely
on instrument-level variables to predict LGDs, may not be adequate.
We illustrate the distribution of ﬁtted LGDs under e = 1.0E/C0 11
and e = 0.05 in Figs. 3.1 and 3.2 , respectively. The distribution in
Fig. 3.1 is strongly bi-modal, with large spikes at both ends. In fact,
this distribution shows a stronger bi-modal pattern than the actual
LGD distribution. In contrast, the distribution in Fig. 3.2 shows a
rather weak bi-modal pattern, quite similar to that of FRR in
Fig. 2 . Since the model ﬁt associated with Fig. 3.1 is rather poor,
these ﬁgures suggest that models that can generate a strong bi-
modal pattern in ﬁtted LGDs do not necessary lead to good model
ﬁt or accurate prediction.
We also study the scatter plots of the actual versus the ﬁtted LGDs
from both transformation methods. The ﬁtted LGDs using
e = 1.0E/C0 11 do not align well with the actual LGDs, with most of
the ﬁtted values deviating substantially away from the 45/C176line. With
e = 0.05, the ﬁtted LGDs are more clustered along the 45/C176line, consis-
tent with the higherR-squared and lower SSE frome = 0.05. The scat-
ter plots of actual and ﬁtted LGDs for the transformation methods
under e = 0.05 are quite similar to those from FRR. These scatter plots
are not reported due to space limit and are available upon request.

4.5. Neural network (NN)
We report results from NN in Table 7 . To choose the optimal n,
the number of hidden-layer units, we divide the sample randomly
into three parts: training, validation and test sets, each represent-
ing 70%, 15%, and 15% of the entire sample. The model ﬁt changes
when n increases. On the training set, R-squared shows tendency
to improve as n becomes larger, whereas on the validation and test
sets, R-squared initially improves then deteriorates as n increases,
with the peak R-squared of 0.556 and 0.576 on the validation and
test sets when n is 25. 14 This suggests that the optimal n for our
sample is likely to be 25, thus we use the same n of 25 to generate
the 10-fold cross-validation results.
NN also shows good 10-fold cross-validation results. The SSE for
the 10-fold cross-validation is the sum of the SSE from each valida-
tion sample, and the sum of SSE is then used to compute the
R-squared for the 10-fold cross-validation. Table 7 shows that the
out-of-sample R-squared of NN is 0.529, which is higher than those
of parametric methods, and the out-of-sample SSE of NN is 259.51,
which is lower than those of parametric methods. Both the
R-squared and SSE have very low standard deviations, indicating
the stable performance of NN out of sample. RT with the minimum
size requirement at 70 or less shows better model ﬁt than NN in
10-fold cross-validation.
We depict the distribution of the ﬁtted LGDs from NN in Fig. 6 .
Although some ﬁtted LGDs are outside the range of [0, 1], these
numbers constitute a fairly small proportion (less than 8%) of the
sample. The distribution of the ﬁtted LGDs is slightly bi-modal,
with higher concentrations in the intervals of [0, 0.1) and
[0.7, 0.8). This result further conﬁrms our earlier ﬁnding that the
distribution of ﬁtted LGDs is not directly related to model
performance. So the bi-modal LGD distribution should be of only
secondary concern in LGD modeling.

5. Further investigations
5.1. Model performance on revolvers and term loans
We also investigate the performance of these six modeling
methods on a smaller sample (1355 observations) consisting of
revolvers and term loans. Results from this sample should be more
relevant to banks’ portfolio and the smaller sample size can also
test the robustness of our earlier ﬁndings. We ﬁnd that our conclu-
sion does not change. The goodness-of-ﬁt from the non-parametric
methods exceeds that from the parametric methods. Second, the
FRR method tends to produce slightly better goodness-of-ﬁt than
the OLS method. Third, performance of the transformation meth-
ods is quite sensitive to the choice of e, and the optimal e is still
around 0.05 for this sample. We have also tried the same methods
on the remaining instruments consisting of bonds. We ﬁnd that our
conclusions hold in this alternative sample as well.
5.2. Model performance when ﬁrm-level variables are included in the
model
In the results reported so far, we do not include ﬁrm-level vari-
ables among the RHS variables. Incorporating these variables sig-
niﬁcantly reduces the sample size to 1267 observations. When
we try the same methods on this smaller sample, our conclusions
about the comparative performance of the various methods to
model LGDs do not change. However, we caution that this conclu-
sion might be speciﬁc to the nature of our sample (i.e., predomi-
nantly bankruptcy cases and large ﬁrms) and this question is
worth further examination on alternative samples in the future.
5.3. Model performance when only dummy variables are included in
the model
Further, in separate analysis, we ﬁnd that non-parametric
methods do not enjoy any advantage relative to parametric
methods when the explanatory variables consist solely of the
instrument type and collateral-type variables – the explanatory
variables commonly used by banks in modeling LGD. 15 There are
two reasons for the inferior performance of the non-parametric
methods in this case. First, in models with only instrument type
and collateral-type variables, the independent variables are all 0/1
dummy variables, with no non-linearity in the model. Consequently,
non-parametric methods do not have any edge. Second, unlike the
non-parametric methods, an intercept term is included in the para-
metric methods, which results in slightly better ﬁt. We therefore
conclude that the advantage enjoyed by non-parametric methods
relative to parametric methods stems from their ability to model
the non-linear relation between LGDs and continuous variables.
Therefore, unless continuous variables are included in the model,
non-parametric methods do not outperform parametric methods,
even with a very large sample size. Modelers of LGD should thus
try to compare performance of alternative parametric and non-
parametric methods on their own data and the set of available
explanatory variables to determine which method to choose.

6. Conclusions
This paper compares various parametric and non-parametric
methods to model and forecast LGD, a continuous random variable
that lies in the interval of [0, 1] and often follows a bi-modal distri-
bution. We include in this study four parametric methods, namely,
the OLS, fractional response, inverse Gaussian, and inverse Gauss-
ian with beta transformation regressions, and two non-parametric
methods, namely, the neural network and regression tree.
We ﬁnd that the non-parametric methods outperform the para-
metric methods in terms of model ﬁt and predictive accuracy. The
neural network method delivers very good model ﬁt both in sam-
ple and in the 10-fold cross-validation. One of the limitations of the
neural network method is that the estimated model is often con-
sidered as a ‘‘black box,’’ since there is no straight forward way
to show the complex non-linear underlying relationships. The
regression tree method also provides very high predictive accu-
racy. After a decent number of splits, the goodness-of-ﬁt from this
method exceeds those from the parametric methods. When we
reduce the minimum size requirement to 70 or less and allow 41
or more splits, the regression tree method provides better ﬁt than
the neural network in the 10-fold cross-validation. Further, the
regression tree does not appear to have the over-ﬁtting problem
even when the minimum size requirement is reduced to 5 with
as many as 342 splits, although such a large tree may not be
practical in a production environment.
Among the parametric methods, fractional response regression
tends to produce slightly better goodness-of-ﬁt than the OLS
method, and both the OLS and the fractional response regression
methods are able to provide decent model ﬁt. Both transformation
methods are very sensitive to the choice of
e (which is needed to
transform LGD = 0 or 1). With an optimal e, their performance
can match that of the OLS regression. Therefore, users of these
transformation methods need to exercise great caution.
Further, we ﬁnd that the bi-modal distribution may be of only
secondary concern when modeling LGD. Finally, inferences on
some instrument-level variables are slightly different between
the models, suggesting that the instrument-level variables may
not have a clear-cut relation with LGD.
